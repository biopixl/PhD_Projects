\title{Rapid detection of metal contamination in ash on outdoor surfaces after the 2025 Eaton Fire in a Wildland-Urban Interface}

% ============================================================================
% ABSTRACT (150-200 words)
% ============================================================================

\begin{abstract}
Wildland–Urban Interface (WUI) fires combust a heterogeneous mix of vegetation and urban materials, generating ash that can contain elevated concentrations of toxic metals, posing potential post-fire exposure risks. Despite increasing frequency and severity of WUI fires, metal contamination in coarse outdoor ash remains poorly characterized, and rapid screening tools suitable for appropriate emergency response are limited. Here, we quantified the concentration and spatial variability of metals in ash collected from outdoor impervious surfaces following the 2025 Eaton Fire in Los Angeles County, California, and evaluated X‑ray fluorescence (XRF) spectroscopy for rapid field screening of lead (Pb). Thirty‑nine ash samples were analyzed by inductively coupled plasma mass spectrometry (ICP‑MS), and paired subsamples were used to develop site‑specific XRF calibration models. Lead concentrations ranged from 14 to 18,528 ppm and exhibited extreme spatial heterogeneity (coefficient of variation = 464\%), with the highest concentrations from samples collected within the urban burn perimeter. Empirical calibration using cumulative Pb L‑line intensities showed strong in-sample correlation with ICP‑MS ($R^2$ = 0.994); however, leave-one-out cross-validation revealed reduced generalization to held-out samples ($R^2_{cv}$ = 0.84; $RMSE_{cv}$ = 1,232 ppm), with model stability sensitive to high-concentration samples. Bland-Altman analysis showed 95\% limits of agreement from $-$704 to +532 ppm. Despite these quantitative limitations, binary threshold classification was robust: when evaluated against regulatory screening thresholds, the calibrated model achieved 100\% sensitivity and specificity at $\geq$400 ppm, though sensitivity declined to 86\% at 200 ppm and 70\% at 80 ppm. These results demonstrate that site‑calibrated portable XRF reliably \textit{classifies} ash relative to high-level regulatory thresholds ($\geq$400 ppm) but should not be used for quantitative Pb determination or screening at lower thresholds without ICP-MS confirmation. We recommend reporting cross-validated metrics and threshold-specific classification performance when validating field screening tools for regulatory applications.\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

% Introduction should establish context, identify knowledge gaps, and state objectives
% Typically 3-5 paragraphs, ~500-800 words

The Wildland-Urban Interface (WUI) is the transition zone between wildland vegetation and the urban build environment, characterized by a complex mosaic of land cover and land use.\cite{radeloff2018wui} Fires in the WUI are fueled by a heterogeneous pre‑fire mosaic that includes vegetation (e.g., evergreen forest and chaparral) as well as manufactured materials from housing, vehicles, and urban infrastructure. In contrast to non‑WUI wildfires, which are fueled predominantly by vegetation, WUI fires involve the partial or complete combustion of anthropogenic materials such as structures and vehicles, resulting in the release of toxic compounds, including heavy metals.\cite{young2004hazards,jech2024marshall,villarruel2024bioaccessible,pereira2010heavymetals,fernandezmarcos2022toxics,terzano2021fireeffects,abraham2017postfire,alshehri2023nanomaterials,alexakis2020suburban} Despite their increasing frequency and severity, the environmental impacts of WUI wildfires remain poorly constrained, as highlighted by a recent U.S. National Academies report.\cite{nasem2022wuichemistry} Metals present in pre‑fire WUI landscapes can volatilize at high combustion temperatures and subsequently condense onto ash particle surfaces during cooling.\cite{senior2000vaporization} The contamination potential of wildfire ash is influenced by burn severity, fuel type, ash load, post‑fire climatic conditions, surface hydrology, and ash physical properties.\cite{sanchezgarcia2023ashchemistry} Trace metal concentration in ash is highly dependent on the metal content and composition of the combusted materials.\cite{pereira2010heavymetals,abraham2017postfire,etiegni1991woodash,campos2016eucalyptpine} For example, legacy lead‑based paint in pre‑1978 structures increases both the magnitude and spatial extent of lead emissions during WUI fires.\cite{schissel2025emissions} Few studies have focused on metal concentrations in outdoor ash from WUI fires which often burn older structures.

Lead (Pb) is a toxic metal that can cause damage to almost all organs and organ systems, with adverse health effects documented even at very low exposure levels.\cite{tong2000leadexposure,jusko2008bloodlead,levin2008leadexposures} The U.S. Environmental Protection Agency (EPA) Regional Screening Levels (RSLs) for residential soil set a Pb threshold of 200 ppm, while California's Total Threshold Limit Concentration (TTLC) for hazardous waste classification is 1000 ppm.\cite{epa2024residential,dtsc2024ttlc} Despite increasing WUI fire frequency, no standardized field assessment protocol exists for ash contamination on outdoor public surfaces, and laboratory analysis via ICP-MS requires days to weeks---a delay during which ash remains mobile and accessible to returning residents.

The 2025 Eaton Fire, a WUI fire in Los Angeles County, was the second most destructive fire in California's history destroying over 9,000 structures in the foothill communities of the San Gabriel Mountains including Altadena, Kinneloa Mesa, and Sierra Madre. The fire ignited on January 7, 2025, and expanded to over 10,600 within 24 hours, with most of the catastrophic damage occurring gin the first 36 hours \cite{calfire2025eaton}. An unusually extreme Santa Ana wind event forecasted by the National Weather Society realized sustained winds above 90 MPH and wind gusts over 110 MPH descending from the canyons.\cite{nws2025wind} No progress on containment was reported until January 12 as the fire perimeter expanded northward into the San Gabriel Mountains. The Eaton Fire lasted 24 days (January 7-31, 2025) and burned an area larger than 14,000 acres leading to an estimated \$27.5 billion USD in damages.

The smoke plume from the Eaton Fire travelled South-West and deposited coarse, residual ashes for miles over the western coastline. Similar WUI fires have been studied to understand the post-fire environmental contamination of ash and soil with toxic concentrations of metals.\cite{jech2024marshall,villarruel2024bioaccessible} During the 2025 Los Angeles Fires, air quality monitors positioned miles away from the Eaton fire recorded a 110-fold increase in the hourly lead (Pb) concentrations of particulate matter particles with a diameter less than 2.5 µm (PM2.5) compared to pre-fire days.\cite{baliaka2025mmwr} This collaborative effort emerged to respond to a gap in our knowledge about the composition and distribution of metals in the coarse ash fraction remaining on outdoor surfaces of the post-fire landscape. 

We conducted extensive, systematic sampling of outdoor ash accumulated on impervious surfaces (e.g., sidewalk, curbs) both within the burn perimeter and downwind areas where no structures were destroyed. We hypothesized that the ash samples collected within the burn perimeter would contain higher concentrations of Pb and other metals due to proximity to combusted urban infrastructure. The friable nature of ash samples in the field necessitated rapid sampling to avoid areas impacted by cleanup activities and prior to rainfall. This study of the 2025 Eaton Fire provides a framework for assessing potential outdoor environmental exposure to ash from WUI fires. This study addresses three objectives: (1) quantify the concentration and spatial variability of metals in outdoor ash following the 2025 Eaton Fire; (2) develop and evaluate site-specific X-ray fluorescence (XRF) calibration models for Pb; and (3) assess the utility of calibrated XRF measurements for rapid screening against regulatory Pb thresholds.

\section{Materials and Methods}

The sampling campaign was initiated on January 16, 2025, with most samples collected prior to the first post-fire rainfall. Sampling was conducted by teams from Caltech and JPL, which initially sampled independently but coordinated efforts early in the campaign. A 1 × 1 mile grid overlay was developed to guide spatial sampling, with each grid cell assigned a unique numeric identifier. Using this grid to guide a spatially even sampling approach, teams traveled between grid cells collecting ash samples within the burn scar as well as along the western, southern, and eastern flanks of the Eaton Fire perimeter. Within each grid cell, the specific sample sites were selected based on three criteria: (1) public accessibility, (2) presence of visible ash accumulation, and (3) minimal post-fire disturbance from cleanup activities. 

A total of 169 physical samples were collected directly by field teams (95 by JPL and 74 by Caltech), supplemented by an additional 52 samples contributed from Caltech community volunteers. Because sampling began approximately two weeks after ignition, ash had largely accumulated in small wind-traps on impervious surfaces (e.g., on sidewalks against a fence or along street gutters). At each site, areas of concentrated ash deposition were identified, and approximately 5-15 mL of ash was collected. Ash was collected using small, non-metallic tools to minimize contamination (Figure 2A). For each sample, both coarse ash fragments and fine soot or dust were collected to obtain a representative composite sample (Figure 2B). Although efforts were made to collect only post-fire ash material, some mixing with background dust was unavoidable.

Thirty-nine ash samples were selected for their spatial distribution and temporal representativeness (pre-rainfall) for elemental analysis by Inductively Coupled Plasma Mass Spectrometry (ICP-MS); 37 had paired XRF measurements available for the validation analysis below. Bulk ash samples were pulverized by mortar and pestle to homogenize particle sizes <250 µm (Figure 2C). Paired subsamples were aliquoted for parallel Pb content analysis with a benchtop energy-dispersive X-ray Fluorescence (XRF) spectrometer (INAM Expert 3L, RPE Institute of Analytical Control Methods, LLC). The instrument is equipped with a high-resolution Silica Drift Detector and an X-ray source tube (Rh anode, 50 kV max voltage, 1000 µA max current). Each sample was prepared in two ways where material allowed: as a homogenized loose powder mounted in a polymer film cup ($n=54$ preparations) and as a pressed pellet ($n=53$). For pellet preparation, ash subsamples were ground and sieved to <106 µm, mixed with cellulose binder, and pressed into 13-mm pellets (Figure 2D). XRF spectra were acquired over 60 seconds of live time with automatic peak deconvolution and matrix correction via the instrument's fundamental parameters (FP) software, which reported semi-quantitative concentrations in mass fraction percent (m.f.\%). For each measurement we additionally retained the per-line emission intensity (counts s$^{-1}$) for Pb L$\alpha_1$, L$\alpha_2$, L$\beta_{1\textrm{--}4}$, L$\gamma_1$, and L$\ell$ for explicit modeling.

\subsection*{Two-stage calibration}

Pb concentration in ash was estimated using a two-stage calibration approach: (Stage A) a linear calibration of XRF response against known Pb concentrations in clay reference standards (PBP01--PBP04 series with certified Pb at 100, 500, 1000, and 0~ppm; three replicates per series per preparation method, $n=12$ pellet and $n=11$ powder); and (Stage B) an ash-matrix correction that absorbed the systematic clay-to-ash transfer bias arising from the different mass-attenuation coefficients of clay (Si/Al-rich) and ash (Si/Ca/Fe-rich) at Pb-line energies. Four canonical calibration arms were carried forward — pellet/powder $\times$ FP-derived concentration / line intensity. The detailed Stage A calibration of the four arms, including coefficient estimates, leave-one-out cross-validation (LOOCV) of the clay calibration, and the empirical line-selection sweep that motivated the L$\beta$-family choice, is in the Supporting Information.

\subsection*{Spectral line selection}

Pb L-lines were screened a priori for spectral overlap with elements abundant in the ash matrix, then validated empirically. Pb L$\alpha_1$ (10.55~keV) and L$\alpha_2$ (10.45~keV) overlap As K$\alpha$ (10.54~keV); Pb L$\gamma_1$ (14.76~keV) overlaps Rb K$\beta$, Y K$\alpha$, and Sr K$\beta$ in the 14.7--15.8~keV region; and Pb L$\ell$ (9.18~keV) sits in a low-SNR region near Ge/W/Au L-lines. The Pb L$\beta_{1\text{--}4}$ family (12.3--12.8~keV) is interference-free in this matrix. An empirical sweep of 22 candidate response variables (single lines, sums, and multi-predictor combinations; Supporting Information Table~S3) confirmed the a priori choice: the L$\beta_1$+L$\beta_2$ multi-predictor model produced the lowest validation RMSE for pellet, while L$\beta_1$+L$\beta_3$ produced the lowest RMSE for powder. Notably, Pb L$\alpha_1$ — the most intense Pb line and the traditional choice — produced acceptable but inferior performance for pellet (validation RMSE 1080 vs.\ 478~ppm for L$\beta_1$+L$\beta_2$ under all-sample LOOCV) and collapsed for powder (validation RMSE 3102~ppm; $r=0.35$), reflecting the As K$\alpha$ interference at 10.54~keV.

\subsection*{Ash-matrix correction and outlier handling}

For each calibration arm, the ash-matrix correction was a proportional regression $\mathrm{Pb}_{\mathrm{ICP\text{-}MS}} = m_{\mathrm{arm}} \cdot \mathrm{Pb}_{\mathrm{clay\text{-}pred}}$ fit on paired ash samples. The slope $m_{\mathrm{arm}}$ absorbs the clay-to-ash transfer (full-fit values: 1.13 pellet$_{\mathrm{FP}}$, 2.71 pellet$_{\mathrm{intensity}}$, 0.33 powder$_{\mathrm{FP}}$, 0.70 powder$_{\mathrm{intensity}}$; Supporting Information Table~S4). Cook's distance was computed for the proportional regression\cite{cook1977detection}; samples exceeding the conventional threshold $D_i > 4/n$ were excluded from slope-fitting but retained in validation. The same rule was applied uniformly across all four arms. Excluded samples in this study were XPAH28 (ICP-MS Pb~=~18,528~ppm; Cook's $D > 350$ in every arm) and JPL73 (804~ppm); XPAH20 (1,933~ppm) was additionally excluded for the FP arms. A sensitivity analysis evaluating seven outlier-handling strategies (no exclusion, fixed concentration cutoffs, and Cook's distance) is provided in Supporting Information Table~S5; Cook's distance reduced validation RMSE 3-fold for the intensity arms while preserving threshold sensitivity, and naive concentration-based dropping degraded performance.

\subsection*{Validation and statistical analysis}

The matrix-correction slope was leave-one-out cross-validated among eligible samples: when predicting sample $i$, the slope was refit on the eligible subset minus $\{i\}$. Outlier-excluded samples were predicted using the eligible-set full-fit slope (extrapolation). Validation metrics were computed on the complete paired set ($n=32$--36 per arm). LOOCV was preferred over 67/33 holdout and 5-fold cross-validation; with $n \le 36$, holdout splits were dominated by whether XPAH28 fell in the training or test partition, producing standard deviations comparable to means (Supporting Information Table~S6).

Three families of statistics characterize each arm. (1) \textit{Correlation and error}: Pearson $r$, $R^2$, root-mean-square error (RMSE), and mean absolute error (MAE) on the validation set. (2) \textit{Method agreement (Bland-Altman)}: bias and 95\% limits of agreement (LOA $=$ bias~$\pm~1.96\,\sigma$) on absolute differences (ICP-MS minus XRF-predicted, in~ppm), percent differences, and the geometric ratio of ICP-MS to predicted Pb.\cite{bland1986statistical,giavarina2015understanding} Bland-Altman is reported alongside $R^2$ because two methods may be highly correlated yet show systematic bias and wide LOA that preclude interchangeability near regulatory decision thresholds. (3) \textit{Threshold classification}: sensitivity, specificity, and accuracy at six soil/ash regulatory thresholds spanning California and federal screening levels — 80, 200, 320, 500, 800, and 1000~ppm.\cite{epa2024residential,dtsc2024ttlc}

The complete pipeline — sample data cleaning, calibration, matrix correction, validation, and table/figure generation — is implemented in R and Python and is reproducible from a single orchestrator script.

\section{Results}

ICP-MS analysis quantified concentrations of more than 50 elements in the 39 analyzed ash samples, including major elements from biomass combustion residue (Table 1). The elemental concentrations and between-sample variability is summarized in Figure 3. Calcium was the most abundant element (mean 52,728 ppm), followed by iron (36,577 ppm) and aluminum (36,613 ppm) at comparable concentrations (Table 1). Potassium, magnesium, and sodium ranged from 13,000–19,000 ppm. Titanium (6,138 ppm) and manganese (962 ppm) were present at lower concentrations. Among major elements, calcium and magnesium exhibited the highest variability (CV = 116\% and 125\%, respectively), whereas potassium was the most uniformly distributed (CV = 31\%). 

ICP-MS analysis also quantified concentrations of hazardous metals including lead, zinc, copper, and cobalt (Table 2). Zinc concentrations ranged from 100 to 23,829 ppm (mean 1,310 ppm), while lead ranged from 14 to 18,528 ppm (mean 638 ppm). Copper concentrations spanned 14–3,650 ppm (mean 207 ppm). Vanadium, chromium, nickel, and cobalt were present at mean concentrations ranging from 22–85 ppm, while antimony averaged 9.1 ppm and arsenic and cadmium remained below 7 ppm and 1 ppm respectively, across all samples. Lead, zinc, and copper exhibited pronounced spatial heterogeneity (CV = 464\%, 292\%, and 284\%), with maximum concentrations exceeding mean values by one to two orders of magnitude.

Four calibration arms were validated against ICP-MS using the two-stage approach described in Methods: pellet/powder preparation $\times$ FP-derived concentration / line-intensity response. Stage A (clay calibration on PBP standards) and the empirical line-selection sweep are summarized in Supporting Information \S{}1; the final clay-calibration coefficients and Stage A leave-one-out RMSE for the four arms are reported in Table~S2. Stage B (ash-matrix correction) and Stage C (validation against the ICP-MS gold standard) are reported here.

The proportional ash-matrix correction slopes were 1.13 (pellet$_{\mathrm{FP}}$), 2.71 (pellet$_{\mathrm{intensity}}$), 0.33 (powder$_{\mathrm{FP}}$), and 0.70 (powder$_{\mathrm{intensity}}$). LOOCV slope variation was tight for the intensity arms (SD~$\le$~0.10) and slightly higher for the FP arms (SD~$\le$~0.06). XPAH28 (ICP-MS Pb~=~18,528~ppm) and JPL73 (804~ppm) had Cook's $D$ values one to two orders of magnitude above any other sample and were excluded from slope-fitting; XPAH20 (1,933~ppm) was additionally excluded for the FP arms. The full sensitivity analysis comparing seven outlier-handling strategies is in Supporting Information Table~S5.

Validation against ICP-MS revealed substantial differences among the four arms despite all achieving high correlation with the gold standard ($r \ge 0.98$ for every arm; Table~3). The pellet-intensity arm showed the strongest performance: $r = 0.999$, validation RMSE 155~ppm, MAE 69~ppm, Bland-Altman bias $-1$~ppm, and LOA $[-309, +308]$~ppm. The powder-intensity arm followed at $r = 0.998$, RMSE 271~ppm, MAE 111~ppm, BA bias $+36$~ppm, and LOA $[-498, +569]$~ppm. The two FP arms showed similarly high correlations ($r = 0.99$ pellet, $r = 0.98$ powder) but markedly wider Bland-Altman LOA (range 6,733 and 7,056~ppm respectively, $\sim$10$\times$ the intensity-arm LOA range) and persistent under-prediction biases of $+293$ to $+303$~ppm. This divergence between $r$ and Bland-Altman agreement underscores that correlation alone is an insufficient performance metric: two methods may be linearly related yet differ by ppm magnitudes that exceed regulatory decision thresholds. Validation results are visualized in Figure~4 (predicted vs.\ ICP-MS) and the Bland-Altman agreement plots in Figure~5.

Threshold classification at six regulatory and screening concentrations (80, 200, 320, 500, 800, 1000~ppm; Table~4) confirms the suitability of the calibrated XRF for binary screening at the higher thresholds. Both intensity arms achieved 100\% sensitivity, specificity, and accuracy at the 320 and 800~ppm thresholds. The pellet-intensity arm additionally achieved 91\% sensitivity at the 80~ppm California residential RSL with 91\% specificity. A single near-boundary sample at the 500~ppm threshold contributed a false negative across all four arms (sensitivity 0.60--0.80). The FP arms lost sensitivity at the 500~ppm threshold (0.60) due to their systematic under-prediction bias and lost specificity at the 80~ppm threshold (43--80\% specificity, reflecting false positives at the calibration's lower limit). At the highest thresholds (800 and 1000~ppm), all four arms achieved $\ge$94\% accuracy with no false negatives.

\section{Discussion}

This study provides three advances for understanding and managing metal contamination following WUI fires. First, we document Pb exceedances in \textit{outdoor ash on impervious surfaces}---a matrix distinct from the soil and indoor ash assessed in previous WUI fire studies.\cite{jech2024marshall,villarruel2024bioaccessible} This distinction has practical importance: outdoor ash on sidewalks, driveways, and streets represents a direct exposure pathway for residents returning to evacuated areas before formal cleanup is complete. Second, we demonstrate that ash contamination extends \textit{beyond the burn perimeter}, with samples downwind of the fire exceeding RSL thresholds despite no local structure combustion. This finding expands the spatial extent of potential exposure risk beyond the typical focus on burned footprints. Third, we develop and validate a \textit{two-stage XRF calibration} that anchors quantitation in clay reference standards (Stage A) and explicitly corrects the clay-to-ash matrix transfer (Stage B), achieving validation RMSE of 155~ppm and 100\% sensitivity and specificity at the 320 and 800~ppm thresholds for the pellet-intensity arm — performance suitable for both rapid triage and quantitative confirmation across the regulatory decision range.

The relative uniformity of lithogenic elements (Al, Fe, Ti, K; CV = 31--55\%) provides an internal geochemical reference for evaluating pyrogenic enrichment. These elements derive primarily from native bedrock and background soil incorporated into ash during fire progression, and their concentrations showed minimal dependence on sample location relative to the burn perimeter. In contrast, Pb, Zn, and Cu exhibited CV exceeding 280\%, with concentrations spanning three orders of magnitude and strong spatial dependence on proximity to combusted structures. This dichotomy---uniform geogenic background versus heterogeneous pyrogenic signal---confirms that the extreme metal variability reflects point-source combustion emissions from urban materials rather than underlying geological variation.\cite{jech2024marshall,villarruel2024bioaccessible,fernandezmarcos2022toxics,abraham2017postfire} The uniformity of lithogenic elements also suggests that sample preparation and analytical methods were consistent across the dataset; matrix-related analytical artifacts would be expected to introduce variation in all elements, not selectively in those associated with anthropogenic sources.

WUI fires are complex disasters that contaminate multiple environmental media including air, land, and water. Despite the documented toxicity of Pb at low exposure levels,\cite{tong2000leadexposure,jusko2008bloodlead,levin2008leadexposures} wildfire evacuation and reoccupancy guidelines do not uniformly address ash-borne metal exposure---a gap evident in the variable guidance issued following recent California WUI fires including the Camp Fire (2018), Marshall Fire (2021), and Eaton Fire (2025). Our detection of Pb concentrations exceeding California TTLC (1000 ppm) within the burn perimeter---and exceeding EPA RSLs (200 ppm) in downwind areas outside the burn perimeter---demonstrates that exposure risk assessment must consider both the burned footprint and aeolian transport zones. The rapid detection of elevated suspended Pb aerosols during the fire\cite{baliaka2025mmwr} preceded our parallel discovery of high Pb concentrations in outdoor ash, suggesting coordinated air and surface monitoring may provide complementary exposure assessment. For emergency managers, these findings imply that clearance decisions based solely on fire perimeter boundaries may underestimate the spatial extent of contamination requiring assessment or remediation.

Translating laboratory XRF calibrations to field applications requires addressing matrix heterogeneity in ash. Unlike relatively uniform soil matrices, WUI ash comprises variable mixtures of mineral ash, char, glass, and metallic debris from diverse combustion sources. These physical and chemical matrix effects\cite{ravansari2020pxrf} motivated the two-stage calibration approach used here: clay reference standards anchor the linear response of the instrument (Stage A), and a proportional ash-matrix correction absorbs the residual transfer bias when applying the clay-trained model to field samples (Stage B). The matrix-correction slopes (1.13--2.71 across the four arms) quantify a 0.4--3-fold response difference between clay and ash matrices that would otherwise propagate as systematic error.

Two methodological choices were essential for screening-grade performance. First, the empirical line sweep (Supporting Information \S 1) selected interference-free Pb~L$\beta$ multi-predictor models over the traditional Pb~L$\alpha_1$ — a choice the data strongly favored: Pb~L$\alpha_1$ produced acceptable performance for pellet preparations but collapsed for powder ($r = 0.35$, RMSE $> 3000$~ppm), reflecting amplified As~K$\alpha$ interference in the porous powder matrix. Second, Cook's distance--based outlier handling for the matrix-correction step\cite{cook1977detection} reduced validation RMSE 3-fold for the intensity arms relative to all-sample fitting, while preserving threshold sensitivity. Naive concentration-based exclusion (e.g., truncating at 1000~ppm) systematically degraded performance, indicating that high-concentration samples are not inherently problematic; rather, samples whose product of leverage and residual is anomalous are.

The resulting validation performance (Table 3) demonstrates that high $R^2$ alone is an insufficient performance metric. All four arms achieved $r \ge 0.98$, yet their Bland-Altman LOA ranges differ by an order of magnitude — 616~ppm for pellet-intensity vs.\ 7,035~ppm for pellet-FP. The tightest LOA $[-309, +308]$~ppm and the near-zero bias of pellet-intensity make it the only arm whose 95\% agreement interval lies entirely within the 320~ppm threshold band, formally supporting interchangeability with ICP-MS for screening decisions in this concentration range.\cite{bland1986statistical}

These findings support a tiered decision framework for post-fire response. The pellet-intensity arm (Pb~L$\beta_1$+L$\beta_2$ multi-predictor, two-stage calibration with Cook's distance--filtered matrix correction) is recommended as the primary screening method, achieving 100\% sensitivity, specificity, and accuracy at both the 320 and 800~ppm thresholds. For field-deployable screening with portable XRF, the powder-intensity arm (Pb~L$\beta_1$+L$\beta_3$) maintains the same 100\% classification performance at 320 and 800~ppm with somewhat broader agreement (LOA range 1,067~ppm). At the lower 80~ppm California residential RSL, sensitivity is 91\% (pellet) to 82\% (powder); samples in this concentration band should still be referred to ICP-MS for clearance decisions. The 500~ppm threshold sits near a single sample boundary at which all four arms produced one false negative; samples in the 400--600~ppm range warrant confirmatory analysis. We recommend that future XRF validation studies report Bland-Altman bias and limits of agreement at every reported threshold alongside $R^2$ and RMSE, since correlation alone cannot establish suitability for regulatory decision-making.
