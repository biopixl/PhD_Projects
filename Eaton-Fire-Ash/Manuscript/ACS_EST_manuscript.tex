\title{Rapid detection of metal contamination in ash on outdoor surfaces after the 2025 Eaton Fire in a Wildland-Urban Interface}

% ============================================================================
% ABSTRACT (150-200 words)
% ============================================================================

\begin{abstract}
Wildland–Urban Interface (WUI) fires combust a heterogeneous mix of vegetation and urban materials, generating ash that can contain elevated concentrations of toxic metals, posing potential post-fire exposure risks. Despite increasing frequency and severity of WUI fires, metal contamination in coarse outdoor ash remains poorly characterized, and rapid screening tools suitable for appropriate emergency response are limited. Here, we quantified the concentration and spatial variability of metals in ash collected from outdoor impervious surfaces following the 2025 Eaton Fire in Los Angeles County, California, and evaluated X‑ray fluorescence (XRF) spectroscopy for rapid field screening of lead (Pb). Thirty‑nine ash samples were analyzed by inductively coupled plasma mass spectrometry (ICP‑MS), and paired subsamples were used to develop site‑specific XRF calibration models. Lead concentrations ranged from 14 to 18,528 ppm and exhibited extreme spatial heterogeneity (coefficient of variation = 459\%), with the highest concentrations from samples collected within the urban burn perimeter. Empirical calibration using cumulative Pb L‑line intensities achieved strong agreement with ICP‑MS after leave-one-out cross-validation ($r$ = 0.999; RMSE = 151~ppm). Bland-Altman analysis showed 95\% limits of agreement from $-$302 to +300~ppm (range = 602~ppm). Despite these quantitative limitations, binary threshold classification was robust: when evaluated against regulatory screening thresholds, the calibrated model achieved 100\% sensitivity and specificity at $\geq$320 ppm, though sensitivity declined to 86\% at 200 ppm and 91\% at 80 ppm. These results demonstrate that site‑calibrated portable XRF reliably \textit{classifies} ash relative to regulatory thresholds ($\geq$320 ppm) but should not be used for quantitative Pb determination or screening at lower thresholds without ICP-MS confirmation. We recommend reporting cross-validated metrics and threshold-specific classification performance when validating field screening tools for regulatory applications.\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

% Introduction should establish context, identify knowledge gaps, and state objectives
% Typically 3-5 paragraphs, ~500-800 words

The Wildland-Urban Interface (WUI) is the transition zone between wildland vegetation and built urban environments, characterized by heterogenous land cover and land use.\cite{radeloff2018wui} Fires in the WUI are fueled by a heterogeneous pre‑fire mosaic that includes vegetation (e.g., evergreen forest and chaparral) as well as manufactured materials from housing, vehicles, and urban infrastructure. In contrast to non‑WUI wildfires, which are fueled predominantly by vegetation, WUI fires involve the partial or complete combustion of anthropogenic materials such as structures and vehicles, resulting in the release of toxic compounds, including heavy metals.\cite{young2004hazards,jech2024marshall,villarruel2024bioaccessible,pereira2010heavymetals,fernandezmarcos2022toxics,terzano2021fireeffects,abraham2017postfire,alshehri2023nanomaterials,alexakis2020suburban} Despite their increasing frequency and severity, the environmental impacts of WUI wildfires remain poorly constrained, as highlighted by a recent U.S. National Academies report.\cite{nasem2022wuichemistry} Metals present in pre‑fire WUI landscapes can volatilize at high combustion temperatures and subsequently condense onto ash particle surfaces during cooling.\cite{senior2000vaporization} The contamination potential of wildfire ash is influenced by burn severity, fuel type, ash load, post‑fire climatic conditions, surface hydrology, and ash physical properties.\cite{sanchezgarcia2023ashchemistry} Trace metal concentration in ash is highly dependent on the metal content and composition of the combusted materials.\cite{pereira2010heavymetals,abraham2017postfire,etiegni1991woodash,campos2016eucalyptpine} For example, legacy lead‑based paint in pre‑1978 structures increases both the magnitude and spatial extent of lead emissions during WUI fires.\cite{schissel2025emissions} Few studies have focused on metal concentrations in outdoor ash from WUI fires which often burn older structures.

Lead (Pb) is a toxic metal that can cause damage to almost all organs and organ systems, with adverse health effects documented even at very low exposure levels.\cite{tong2000leadexposure,jusko2008bloodlead,levin2008leadexposures} The U.S. Environmental Protection Agency (EPA) Regional Screening Levels (RSLs) for residential soil set a Pb threshold of 200 ppm, while California's Total Threshold Limit Concentration (TTLC) for hazardous waste classification is 1000 ppm.\cite{epa2024residential,dtsc2024ttlc} Despite increasing WUI fire frequency, no standardized field assessment protocol exists for ash contamination on outdoor public surfaces. Laboratory analysis via ICP-MS requires days to weeks, a delay during which ash remains mobile and accessible to returning residents.

The 2025 Eaton Fire in Los Angeles County destroyed over 9,000 structures in the foothill communities of Altadena, Kinneloa Mesa, and Sierra Madre, making it the second most destructive fire in California history.\cite{calfire2025eaton} Extreme Santa Ana winds (gusts $>$100~mph) drove rapid fire spread, with most structural damage occurring within 36 hours of ignition on January 7, 2025.\cite{nws2025wind}

The smoke plume from the Eaton Fire travelled South-West and deposited coarse, residual ashes for miles over the western coastline. Similar WUI fires have been studied to understand the post-fire environmental contamination of ash and soil with toxic concentrations of metals.\cite{jech2024marshall,villarruel2024bioaccessible} During the 2025 Los Angeles Fires, air quality monitors positioned miles away from the Eaton fire recorded a 110-fold increase in the hourly lead (Pb) concentrations of particulate matter particles with a diameter less than 2.5 µm (PM2.5) compared to pre-fire days.\cite{baliaka2025mmwr} The present study addresses a gap in our knowledge of the composition and distribution of metals in the coarse ash fraction remaining on outdoor surfaces following WUI fires. 

We conducted extensive, systematic sampling of outdoor ash accumulated on impervious surfaces (e.g., sidewalk, curbs) both within the burn perimeter and downwind areas where no structures were destroyed. We hypothesized that the ash samples collected within the burn perimeter would contain higher concentrations of Pb and other metals due to proximity to combusted urban infrastructure. The friable nature of ash samples in the field necessitated rapid sampling to avoid areas impacted by cleanup activities and prior to rainfall. Using the 2025 Eaton Fire as a case study, we provide a framework for assessing potential outdoor environmental exposure to ash from WUI fires. Specifically, we address three objectives: (1) quantify the concentration and spatial variability of metals in outdoor ash following the 2025 Eaton Fire; (2) develop and evaluate site-specific X-ray fluorescence (XRF) calibration models for Pb; and (3) assess the utility of calibrated XRF measurements for rapid screening against regulatory Pb thresholds.

\section{Materials and Methods}

Ash sampling started on January 16\textsuperscript{th}, 2025, coordinated early by efforts from both the California Institute of Technology (Caltech) and the National Aeronautics and Space Administration’s Jet Propulsion Laboratory (JPL). Using a 1 x 1 mile grid to guide a spatially even sampling approach, teams traveled between grid cells collecting ash samples within the burn scar as well as along the western, southern, and eastern flanks of the Eaton Fire perimeter. Within each grid cell, the specific sample sites were selected based on three criteria: (1) public accessibility, (2) presence of visible ash accumulation, and (3) absence of debris removal and cleanup activities. 

A total of 169 physical samples were collected directly by field teams (95 by JPL and 74 by Caltech), supplemented by an additional 52 samples contributed from Caltech community volunteers using a standardized sampling kit including protective, sampling, and storage equipment provided by the authors. As sampling began approximately two weeks after ignition, ash had largely accumulated in small wind-traps on impervious surfaces (e.g., on sidewalks against a fence or along street gutters. At each site, areas of concentrated ash deposition were identified, and glass sampling vials were filled with 5-15 mL of ash for storage. Ash was collected using non-metallic tools to minimize contamination (Figure 2A). For each sample, both coarse ash fragments and fine soot or dust was combined to obtain a representative composite sample (Figure 2B). Although sampling targeted post-fire ash material, limited mixing and dilution with background dust was unavoidable; consequently, concentrations should be interpreted as minimum estimates of contamination levels. 

Thirty-eight ash samples (collected 16--25 January 2025) were selected for their spatial distribution and temporal representativeness (pre-rainfall) to proceed with elemental analysis by inductively coupled plasma mass spectrometry (ICP-MS). Per EPA Method 6200 protocols, bulk ash samples were stored dry and non-representative material (e.g., leaves, twigs, etc.) was removed before ash was pulverized with a mortar and pestle, then sieved through a 250 µm mesh to provide an ash powder aliquot to XRF and ICP-MS analysis (Figure 2C) \cite{epa6200}, \cite{epa6020b}. Of these, the 33 samples with sufficient collected material for both powder and pressed pellet preparation constitute the validation set used throughout the XRF calibration analysis described here out, the remaining opportunistic samples with partial XRF and ICPMS coverage are included in the Supplementary Information. 

The 33 fully paired ash samples were aliquoted for Pb analysis with a benchtop energy-dispersive X-ray fluorescence (XRF) spectrometer (INAM Expert 3L, RPE Institute of Analytical Control Methods, LLC) over 60 seconds of exposure. To minimize grain-size effects on XRF spectra, pressed pellet subsamples were homogenized by grinding and sieving to a $<$106~µm grain size, mixed with cellulose binder (Corporation), and pressed into 13-mm diameter pellets (Figure~2D). Rather than relying on the instrument's fundamental-parameters (FP) semi-quantitative output, we developed an empirical calibration using Pb~L$\beta_{1}$ and L$\beta_{2}$ line intensity (counts~s$^{-1}$). This response avoids spectral interference with other elements and was selected from an empirical sweep over single-line, summed-line, and multi-predictor combinations of six Pb L-lines described in the Supplementary Information along with laboratory control spikes and matrix correction used in model calibration. The XRF Pb predictive model using the ash pellet L$\beta_{1}$ and L$\beta_{2}$ response was validated against ICP-MS Pb reference values on the 33 fully paired ash samples. Model performance is reported by Pearson $r$ and root-mean-square error (RMSE) for prediction accuracy, by Bland-Altman bias and 95\% limits of agreement (LOA) for method interchangeability,\cite{bland1986statistical,giavarina2015understanding} and by sensitivity, specificity, and accuracy at six soil-Pb regulatory thresholds (Table 1).

\section{Results}

ICP-MS analysis quantified more than 50 elements across the 38 analyzed ash samples (Complete dataset in Supporting Information Table~S9). Major elements (Ca, Fe, Al) showed low to moderate variability (CV~=~36--115\%), wheras toxic heavy metas (HM) showed extreme spatial heterogeneity. Pb varied most dramatically (CV~=~459\%, range 14--18,528~ppm), followed by zinc (Zn, CV~=~288\%), and copper (Cu, CV~=~281\%), each spanning two to three orders of magnitude across the sample set (Figure~\ref{fig:icpms_summary}). The highest Pb concentrations, along with elevated Co, Cu, and Zn, occured in ashes collected within and southwest of the urban burn perimeter (Figure~\ref{fig:spatial_map}).

Among XRF calibration approaches tested, the pressed-pellet L$\beta_{1}$+L$\beta_{2}$ intensity model showed the strongest agreement with ICP-MS Pb reference values across the 33 fully paired ash samples (Table~\ref{tab:xrf_validation}; Figure~\ref{fig:xrf_validation}): $r = 0.999$, RMSE = 151~ppm, and a Bland-Altman 95\% limits of agreement (LOA) range of 602~ppm with negligible bias ($-$1~ppm). Threshold classification performance is summarized in Table~\ref{tab:threshold_classification}. At the 320~ppm threshold, the pellet intensity model achieved perfect classification (sensitivity = 1.00 [0.61--1.00]; specificity = 1.00 [0.88--1.00]. At the 80~ppm threshold, sensitivity dropped to 0.91 [0.62--0.98], with one false negative and one false positive. Pooled across all six thresholds, the model produced 3 false negatives in 34 above-threshold tests (sensitivity = 0.91 [0.77--0.97]), and 4 false positives in 164 below-threshold tests (specificity = 0.98 [0.94--0.99]). Comparison with alternative calibration approaches are provided in Supporting Information \S 2.

\section{Discussion}

\textit{Metal contamination patterns.}
WUI fires produce ash with a hybrid geochemical signature distinct from natural wildfires that burn only vegetation biomass.\cite{pereira2010heavymetals,etiegni1991woodash,sanchezgarcia2023ashchemistry} After the 2025 Eaton Fire, toxic heavy metals of concern (Pb, Zn, Cu, and Co) were more concentrated in ash collected within the urban burn perimeter than in ash collected outside the burned area, indicating a strong influence of pre-fire urban land cover on post-fire ash composition. In contrast, elements with the highest absolute concentrations across all samples (e.g., Al, Fe, Ti) reflect the regional geological substrate and occur at relatively uniform levels (CV = 31--56\%) consistent with a dominant geogenic origin. These geogenic elements showed low spatial variability, whereas pyrogenic elements derived from combustion of heterogeneous materials from the urban built environment exhibit substantial variability in metal concentration (Pb, Zn, Cu with CV $>$ 280\%). The uniformity of geogenic elements rules out pre-fire soil heterogeneity as the source of metal variability, confirming that combustion of built-environment materials is the dominant contamination driver. Ash samples with elevated Pb extended downwind into areas where no structures burned, demonstrating aeolian transport of contaminated ash beyond the urban fire footprint. These findings are consistent with prior observations of highly variable post-fire metal contamination in residential soils following WUI fires, where combustion involves a mosaic of vegetation and anthropogenic point-source emissions.\cite{jech2024marshall,villarruel2024bioaccessible,abraham2017postfire,fernandezmarcos2022toxics} Because WUI landscapes differ widely in their material composition prior to fires, resulting contaminant mixtures are expected to vary substantially across events and locations.

\textit{Public health implications.}
WUI fires generate complex contaminant mixtures that affect multiple environmental media including air, soil, and water with surface residues such as ash. Currently, wildfire evacuation and reoccupancy guidelines generally emphasize smoke exposure but rarely account explicitly for additional exposure pathways associated with contaminated ash. At present, no standardized framework exists for assessing post-fire environmental habitability based on ash contaminant exposure. Lead is a highly toxic metal capable of causing multisystem health effects even at low exposure levels.\cite{tong2000leadexposure,jusko2008bloodlead,levin2008leadexposures} Two ash samples exceeded the California TTLC (1000~ppm), and samples collected downwind of the urban burn perimeter exceeded the EPA residential RSL (200~ppm). The 110-fold increase in airborne Pb during the fire\cite{baliaka2025mmwr} is consistent with our observation of elevated Pb in deposited ash. Prevailing southwesterly winds during the fire suggest transport of metal-enriched ash derived from combustion of dense urban fuel sources, including residential and commercial structures, electrical infrastructure, and manufactured metal-containing goods such as electronic components and electric vehicles. Combined with air-quality monitoring during the active fire phase, surface-ash screening addresses both inhalation and ingestion exposure pathways.

\textit{XRF screening performance.}
XRF spectroscopy offers rapid, non-destructive elemental quantification but faces analytical challenges in heterogeneous matrices such as wildfire ash. Physical matrix effects related to particle size, density, and moisture content influence X-ray penetration depth and fluorescence yield. Chemical matrix effects arise from interactions among major elements that alter X-ray absorption and secondary fluorescence. Empirical calibration using cumulative Pb L$\beta_1$+L$\beta_2$ intensity overcame many of these limitations. The pellet L-line model achieved 100\% sensitivity and specificity at the 320~ppm threshold, with sensitivity declining to 86\% at 200~ppm and 91\% at 80~ppm (Table~\ref{tab:threshold_classification}). Bland-Altman analysis showed a 95\% LOA range of 602~ppm with negligible bias ($-$1~ppm), indicating method interchangeability for screening purposes. Alternative calibration approaches showed 10$\times$ wider agreement intervals (Supporting Information \S 2).

\textit{Matrix effects on prediction accuracy.}
Despite strong overall model performance, individual samples with unusual matrix compositions showed larger prediction errors. Samples with extremely high calcium content ($>$25\% Ca by mass) exhibited suppressed Pb L-line fluorescence intensity relative to their ICP-MS Pb concentrations. This suppression likely reflects enhanced X-ray absorption by the calcium-dominated matrix, which attenuates both the incident X-ray beam and the emitted Pb L-line fluorescence. Such Ca-rich ashes probably derive from combustion of calcium-containing building materials such as concrete, drywall, or calcium carbonate-based products. The clay-based calibration, anchored to a kaolinite matrix with relatively low Ca content, does not fully account for this absorption effect. Samples with low Pb concentrations ($<$50~ppm) in high-Ca matrices are therefore at greatest risk of false-negative classification. For screening applications, samples visually dominated by calcium-rich material (e.g., white calcium cement or concrete residue) may warrant conservative interpretation or direct ICP-MS confirmation.

\textit{Operational implications.}
Current post-WUI clearance decisions depend on laboratory turnaround times of days to weeks, during which outdoor ash remains mobile and accessible to returning residents. Two operational improvements follow from this work. First, a 60-second XRF measurement on a pressed pellet classifies samples relative to the 320~ppm threshold with 100\% accuracy in the validated set, supporting site-specific re-entry decisions rather than blanket perimeter-based rules. Second, deployment of portable XRF across the post-fire footprint can produce same-day spatial maps of Pb exceedance, enabling triage of areas for expedited remediation ahead of laboratory confirmation of borderline samples.

\textit{Recommendations.}
Based on these findings, we recommend four practices for similar analyses of WUI ash. First, develop site-specific calibration models using paired XRF and ICP-MS measurements that span the expected concentration range. Second, report raw emission line intensities alongside any instrument-derived concentrations to enable cross-instrument recalibration. Third, use the 320~ppm threshold for field triage and refer samples in the 80--320~ppm range to ICP-MS for clearance decisions. Fourth, re-validate calibrations when transferring to other geological settings or fuel mixtures. For field applications where pellet pressing is impractical, loose-powder preparation with Pb L$\beta_1$+L$\beta_3$ offers comparable classification accuracy at 320~ppm with broader agreement intervals (Supporting Information \S 2).

\textit{Limitations and future directions.}
The calibration was anchored to kaolinite-clay reference standards with an empirical ash-matrix transfer correction. Transfer to matrices with substantially different mineral compositions, particularly Ca-rich calcium carbonate-dominated ashes, would benefit from local re-validation or matrix-matched standards. Given the rapid global expansion of the WUI\cite{radeloff2018wui} and increasing frequency of fires that combust built-environment fuels,\cite{nasem2022wuichemistry,schissel2025emissions} the monitoring framework developed here represents one element of a broader toolkit that the WUI era demands. This toolkit should include standard reference materials representative of WUI ash composition, multi-element screening protocols beyond Pb, integration of surface and air-quality monitoring, and regulatory thresholds explicitly designed for mixed-source contamination. The framework reported here demonstrates that operationally deployable ash characterization with explicit regulatory threshold classification is achievable with existing instrumentation when calibration anchors are constructed and validated transparently.
