\begin{abstract}
%==============================================================================

Wildland-urban interface (WUI) fires generate ash deposits with elevated heavy metal concentrations derived from combusted building materials, consumer products, and vehicles. The January 2025 Eaton Fire in Los Angeles County destroyed over 9,400 structures, consuming an estimated 1.6 $\times$ 10\textsuperscript{8} kg of fuel of which 67\% comprised anthropogenic materials. We quantified metal contamination in 39 ash samples collected prior to significant rainfall using inductively coupled plasma mass spectrometry (ICP-MS) and evaluated portable X-ray fluorescence (XRF) as a field screening method. Lead concentrations ranged from 14 to 18,528 mg/kg (median 61 mg/kg), with 15\% of samples exceeding the U.S. Environmental Protection Agency residential screening level of 400 mg/kg. Additional exceedances were observed for cobalt (n=5), copper (n=1), and zinc (n=1). Ash Pb concentrations exceeded concurrent post-fire soil measurements, indicating that primary ash deposits represent concentrated contamination sources that become diluted through soil mixing. XRF measurements exhibited matrix-dependent bias requiring empirical correction (Pb\textsubscript{ICP-MS} = [Pb\textsubscript{XRF} $-$ 104]/3.9), with correction factors specific to ash mineralogy and organic carbon content. These findings provide source-term constraints complementary to emission inventories and establish calibration protocols for rapid field assessment of WUI fire contamination.

\end{abstract}


\section{Introduction}

Expansion of the wildland-urban interface (WUI) across the western United States has increased both the frequency of structure-involved wildfires and the magnitude of associated environmental contamination \citep{Radeloff2018}. WUI fires differ fundamentally from wildland fires in their fuel composition and emission profiles: while wildland fires consume primarily vegetation, WUI fires combust substantial quantities of anthropogenic materials including building components, vehicles, and consumer products \citep{Plumlee2007, Quarles2020}. Structure fire ash consequently contains elevated concentrations of lead (Pb) from legacy paint, pipes, and solder; copper (Cu) and zinc (Zn) from electrical infrastructure and galvanized metals; and chromium (Cr) from plating and pressure-treated lumber \citep{Plumlee2007, Gabos2023}.

Recent WUI fire studies have documented Pb concentrations in structure ash ranging from tens to tens of thousands of mg/kg, with substantial spatial heterogeneity reflecting the distribution of Pb-bearing materials within individual properties \citep{Plumlee2007, Hubbs2023}. The 2021 Marshall Fire in Colorado demonstrated that burned residential structures release heavy metals into surrounding soils, with elevated concentrations of Zn, Cu, Cr, and Pb detected in ash-impacted areas, though concentrations remained below health concern thresholds in that study \citep{Jech2024}. However, characterization of the primary ash source material---rather than post-depositional soil mixing---remains limited for most WUI fire events.

The January 2025 Los Angeles fires, including the Eaton and Palisades fires, represented an unprecedented WUI disaster, destroying over 16,000 structures and consuming an estimated 1.6 $\times$ 10\textsuperscript{8} kg of fuel in the Eaton Fire alone \citep{Schissel2025}. Critically, anthropogenic materials (structures and vehicles) comprised approximately 67\% of total fuel loading, with vegetation accounting for only 33\% \citep{Schissel2025}. This fuel composition fundamentally distinguishes WUI fires from wildland fires and has direct implications for contaminant emissions. Lead emissions from the Eaton Fire were estimated at 4.5 $\times$ 10\textsuperscript{7} g (45 metric tons) when accounting for lead-based paint in pre-1978 housing stock---approximately 30 times higher than estimates excluding legacy paint sources \citep{Schissel2025}. These emission inventories, while valuable for atmospheric modeling, leave open questions regarding the spatial distribution and concentration of metals in deposited ash and the utility of field screening methods for rapid contamination assessment.

Initial soil surveys following the 2025 Los Angeles fires found elevated Pb concentrations in fire-affected areas, with median values of 75--86 mg/kg in Eaton Fire soils compared to background levels of 20--40 mg/kg \citep{Flores2025}. However, soil measurements integrate ash deposits with underlying substrate, potentially diluting or obscuring the primary contamination signal from combusted materials. Direct characterization of deposited ash prior to soil mixing provides essential data for understanding source concentrations, spatial variability, and the relationship between fuel loading and contamination intensity.

Laboratory-based methods including inductively coupled plasma mass spectrometry (ICP-MS) provide accurate metal quantification but require substantial time and resources for sample processing. Portable X-ray fluorescence (XRF) spectrometry offers a rapid field alternative; however, matrix effects in heterogeneous materials such as wildfire ash introduce measurement bias requiring empirical correction \citep{Kalnicky2001, Weindorf2014}. Validation of XRF screening protocols against laboratory standards has not been systematically conducted for WUI fire ash.

This study addresses three knowledge gaps in post-WUI fire contamination assessment: (1) we characterize metal concentrations in deposited ash prior to soil mixing, providing source-term data complementary to emission inventories and post-fire soil surveys; (2) we evaluate portable XRF as a field screening method and develop empirical correction factors specific to WUI fire ash matrices; and (3) we assess the spatial distribution of contamination relative to regulatory screening levels to inform remediation prioritization.

\section{Study Area}
%==============================================================================

The Eaton Fire ignited on January 7, 2025 in Eaton Canyon at the base of the San Gabriel Mountains in Altadena, California (Figure~\ref{fig:study_area}). Santa Ana wind conditions with gusts exceeding 40 m/s drove rapid fire spread, with the perimeter expanding to 4,300 ha within 24 hours. The fire ultimately burned 5,714 ha and destroyed 9,418 structures before containment on January 31, 2025, causing 19 fatalities and ranking as the second most destructive wildfire in California history.

The burn area encompasses the communities of Altadena and portions of Pasadena at elevations of 360--550 m above sea level (34.127--34.207°N, 118.020--118.194°W). Residential development in the study area occurred predominantly between 1920 and 1970 \citep{Manson2022}, corresponding to the period of widespread lead-based paint application (discontinued 1978) and leaded gasoline use (phased out 1996), both recognized sources of legacy Pb contamination in urban soils \citep{Mielke1999, Laidlaw2012}.

%------------------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Geological Setting}
%------------------------------------------------------------------------------

The study area occupies the Raymond Basin at the base of the San Gabriel Mountains, which comprise Precambrian gneiss and Mesozoic granitic rocks uplifted along the Sierra Madre--Cucamonga fault zone \citep{Yerkes1985, Morton2014}. Alluvial fan deposits derived from Eaton Wash and adjacent drainages include Holocene unconsolidated sediments and Pleistocene semi-consolidated materials \citep{Morton2014}.

Granitic basement rocks in the region contain Pb at 10--30 mg/kg, Cu at 10--50 mg/kg, Zn at 40--80 mg/kg, and As at $<$5 mg/kg, consistent with average upper continental crust \citep{Rudnick2003}. Ultramafic lithologies, where present, may contain elevated Cr and Ni concentrations \citep{Oze2004}. Urban soils in the Los Angeles Basin typically exhibit Pb concentrations of 50--200 mg/kg reflecting legacy contamination from leaded gasoline and paint \citep{Mielke2011, Laidlaw2012}.

%------------------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Hydrogeological Context}
%------------------------------------------------------------------------------

The Raymond Basin is a 104 km\textsuperscript{2} alluvial aquifer system bounded by the San Gabriel Mountain front to the north, the Raymond Fault to the south, and the Arroyo Seco to the west \citep{USGS2018}. The aquifer comprises unconsolidated to semi-consolidated deposits with average thickness of 170 m and provides municipal water supply to Pasadena and adjacent communities. Recharge occurs primarily through infiltration in Eaton Wash and Arroyo Seco spreading basins \citep{USGS2018}. The distribution of fire-derived ash across these recharge zones raised concern for potential groundwater quality impacts.

\section{Methods}
%==============================================================================

\subsection{Sample Collection}

Ash samples were collected between January 16--25, 2025, approximately 9--18 days post-ignition and prior to the first significant rainfall on January 26, 2025. A 2.6 $\times$ 2.6 km grid was established for spatial planning, with sampling sites selected based on (1) public accessibility, (2) visible ash accumulation, and (3) absence of post-fire disturbance from remediation activities.

Field teams from the California Institute of Technology (n=74) and Jet Propulsion Laboratory (n=95) collected 169 samples within and adjacent to the burn perimeter, supplemented by 52 samples from community participants using standardized collection protocols. At each site, 5--15 mL of ash was collected from wind-deposited accumulations on impervious surfaces (sidewalks, curbs, driveways). At each sample site, we identified where ash was concentrated and collected 5-15 mL of ash. The JPL team used a small nylon-bristle paint brush and plastic laboratory weight boat and Caltech teams sampled using a wooden spoon and tweezers. For each sample, the teams collected both coarser ash flakes as well as fine soot and dust to achieve a representative sample. Note that while we attempted to only collect burned material, contamination with background dust was impossible to avoid. We also avoided locations where surfaces had been swept, washed, or otherwise altered following the fire event. In some grid cell locations where ash was plentiful, 2-3 samples were taken tens to a few hundred meters apart. Each sample was immediately transferred to a sample vial and the sample identifier, location, person sampling, date, and other miscellaneous notes. 

Community science participants were provided with standardized collection kits including a sampling bag, spoon, and wipe for ash collection. Submitted samples were registered and visually assessed to qualify the sample quantity for appropriate experimental analysis in the lab.

\subsection{Laboratory Analysis}

\subsubsection{Sample Preparation}
We selected a subset of samples to continue analysis as the ICP-MS procedure was too time and resource intensive to analyze all ash samples. We picked a subset that spanned the region (including samples from within the burn scar, downwind to the south, and on the eastern and western flanks). After picking this subset, we split each sample into subsamples for downstream analyses. To obtain representative subsamples from the bulk ash and debris collections, we employed a coning and quartering method on a clean, aluminum-covered bench surface. Each sample was first poured gently onto a large, ceramic plate. Using sanitized plastic scrapers, the material was formed into a central cone, homogenized, and then flattened into a disc. The disc was divided into four equal quarters, and two opposite quarters were recombined, re-coned, and quartered again. This iterative process was repeated until an estimated 1-3g of ash was obtained for downstream processing by grinding and sieving, while the remaining sample fraction was preserved as collected in-situ. Hereafter, we describe the analysis on the subsample processed with grinding and sieving. 

The resulting subsample was transferred onto folded weighing paper and loaded into a clean, labeled microcentrifuge tube with a unique identifier. Each subsample was weighed in a designated clean weighing room using a precision balance, and the mass was recorded. Subsamples were then stored in a dry, dark environment at ambient temperature. 

To standardize sample particle size and remove coarse debris, a second preparation step was carried out under a fume hood with appropriate personal protective equipment and clean lab practices. All tools and surfaces were pre-cleaned with 70\% ethanol, pressurized air, and lint-free wipes (Kimwipes) to minimize contamination. 

The ash subsample was poured into a ceramic mortar and photographed to document particle size and color before processing. Using a ceramic pestle, the ash was ground along the interior surface of the mortar until reduced to a uniform fine powder. 

Ground ash was transferred onto a folded square of weighing paper and returned to its labeled tube for sieving. A clean, square-cut mesh sieve (250 µm) was then mounted between the sample tube and a clean collection tube with two caps that had been punctured and glued together for containing all ash residue within the sample tubes. The sample was inverted and gently rotated to allow finer particles to pass through the mesh, separating the sample into two size fractions: (1) <250 µm, and (2) > 250 µm. Final masses for both fractions were recorded in the weighing room to assess recovery and particle distribution in the fractions. The <250 µm ash subsample was used for laboratory analysis with ICPMS and XRF to limit noise from large, refractory ash species and capture the contributions from the most mobile and bioaccessible ash species. 


\subsubsection{ICP-MS Analysis}

Samples were digested using EPA Method 3051A (microwave-assisted acid digestion) and analyzed by inductively coupled plasma mass spectrometry (ICP-MS) for a suite of 50+ elements including Pb, As, Cd, Cu, Zn, Cr, Ni, and rare earth elements (REEs). Detection limits were typically <0.1 ppm for most trace metals. Quality assurance included analysis of certified reference materials (CRMs), method blanks, and duplicate samples at a frequency of 10\%.

\subsubsection{XRF Analysis}

 XRF measurements were made using a benchtop energy-dispersive X-ray Fluorescence (EDXRF) spectrometer (INAM Expert 3L, INAM Scientific, Iran). The instrument is equipped with a high-resolution Silicon Drift Detector (SDD) and an X-ray tube source (Rh anode, 50 kV max voltage, 1000 µA max current), enabling detection of elements from Na (Z=11) to U (Z=92). Measurements were performed under vacuum to enhance sensitivity for light elements. Spectra were acquired over a 10-minute live time per sample with automatic peak deconvolution and matrix correction via the instrument's fundamental parameters (FP) software package. Calibration for lead quantification was evaluated using kaolinite clay reference materials doped with a lead nitrate salt solution. Quantitative results were reported in mass fraction (\%) as independent elemental abundances, with oxide equivalents calculated post-acquisition for reporting purposes.

\subsubsection{Spectral Characterization}

To evaluate potential spectral proxies for XRF matrix correction and remote contamination assessment, we acquired complementary spectroscopic measurements on a subset of samples. Fourier-transform infrared (FTIR) spectra were collected using attenuated total reflectance (ATR) mode from 4000--400 cm$^{-1}$ for organic functional group identification. Visible-near infrared (VNIR) reflectance spectra (350--2500 nm) were measured using an ASD FieldSpec 4 spectrometer with contact probe. Additionally, AVIRIS-3 airborne imaging spectroscopy data acquired over the burn area on January 15, 2025 were analyzed to evaluate remote sensing indices (dNBR) as contamination predictors. Detailed methods and results for these spectral analyses are presented in the Supplementary Materials.

\subsection{Statistical Analysis}

All statistical analyses were performed in Python using scipy, numpy, and scikit-learn libraries. Spearman rank correlations were used to assess relationships between metal concentrations and potential predictor variables, as metal distributions were non-normal. Linear regression was used to develop XRF correction models, with residual analysis to identify matrix effects. Significance was assessed at $\alpha$ = 0.05 with Bonferroni correction applied for multiple comparisons where appropriate.

Using California's more protective 80 mg/kg residential screening level for Pb, 11 samples (28\%) exceeded this threshold. \subsection{XRF-ICP-MS Correction Model}

Comparison of  XRF and ICP-MS measurements revealed systematic overestimation of Pb by XRF (Figure~\ref{fig:xrf_correction}). Using all data (n = 37), the linear relationship was:

\begin{equation}
\text{Pb}_{\text{XRF}} = 1.71 \times \text{Pb}_{\text{ICP-MS}} + 337 \quad (R^2 = 0.98)
\end{equation}

However, this model was heavily influenced by extreme outliers. After removing 7 statistical outliers identified by the IQR method (samples with Pb > 134 ppm by ICP-MS or XRF > 805 ppm), the relationship for typical samples (n = 30) was:

\begin{equation}
\text{Pb}_{\text{XRF}} = 3.90 \times \text{Pb}_{\text{ICP-MS}} + 104 \quad (R^2 = 0.26)
\end{equation}

The substantially lower R² for the cleaned dataset indicates high variability in XRF measurements for typical ash samples, likely due to matrix effects. The practical correction formula for converting XRF field measurements to ICP-MS equivalent values is:

\begin{equation}
\text{Pb}_{\text{ICP-MS}} \approx \frac{\text{Pb}_{\text{XRF}} - 104}{3.9}
\end{equation}

For field screening purposes, the XRF threshold corresponding to the EPA RSL of 400 ppm is:

\begin{equation}
\text{Pb}_{\text{XRF, threshold}} = 3.90 \times 400 + 104 = 1,662 \text{ ppm}
\end{equation}

\section{Discussion}
%==============================================================================

\subsection{Ash Contamination in Context of WUI Fire Emissions}

The wide range of Pb concentrations observed in Eaton Fire ash (14--18,528 mg/kg) reflects the heterogeneous nature of structure fire ash, where localized ``hot spots'' associated with specific materials (e.g., painted surfaces, batteries, lead pipes) occur within a matrix of lower-contamination ash. The median concentration of 61 mg/kg is below the EPA RSL of 400 mg/kg but exceeds typical geogenic background levels of 10--30 mg/kg, indicating widespread low-level anthropogenic enrichment even in samples not classified as hazardous.

Our ash Pb concentrations are consistent with the emission inventory estimates of \citet{Schissel2025}, who calculated total Pb emissions of 4.5 $\times$ 10\textsuperscript{7} g for the Eaton Fire when accounting for lead-based paint in pre-1978 housing. The substantial uncertainty in that estimate---reflecting limited data on lead paint loading factors and building-age distributions---underscores the value of direct ash measurements for constraining source terms. Our observation that 15\% of samples exceeded the EPA RSL of 400 mg/kg, with maximum concentrations approaching 20,000 mg/kg, confirms that highly contaminated deposits occur within the broader distribution of moderately enriched ash.

Compared to post-fire soil surveys, our ash samples exhibit higher Pb concentrations than the median soil values of 75--86 mg/kg reported by \citet{Flores2025} for Eaton Fire-affected areas. This difference likely reflects dilution of ash with underlying soil substrate in the soil surveys, consistent with the expectation that ash deposits represent a concentrated contamination source that becomes attenuated through mixing with less contaminated substrate. The difference between primary ash concentrations and bulk soil measurements has implications for exposure assessment: direct contact with undiluted ash may pose greater risk than contact with ash-soil mixtures.

\subsection{Comparison with Other WUI Fire Events}

Lead concentrations in Eaton Fire ash span a similar range to those documented in previous WUI fire events. \citet{Plumlee2007} reported Pb concentrations of 4--22,000 mg/kg in ash from the 2007 Southern California wildfires, while \citet{Jech2024} found that burned residential structures in the 2021 Marshall Fire released elevated Zn, Cu, Cr, and Pb into surrounding soils, though bulk soil concentrations remained below health concern thresholds. The Marshall Fire soil measurements are not directly comparable to our ash measurements given matrix differences, but the pattern of multi-metal enrichment is consistent across WUI fire events.

A notable difference between studies concerns the relationship between housing age and contamination. Pre-1978 housing age is a well-established predictor of soil Pb contamination in urban areas \citep{Mielke1999}, yet we found no significant correlation between pre-1978 housing fraction and ash Pb concentrations. Several factors may explain this finding:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Combustion homogenization}: Fire may redistribute and mix materials from different construction eras within a neighborhood
    \item \textbf{Non-paint sources}: Lead in structure fire ash may derive substantially from non-paint sources (pipes, solder, electronics) not strictly correlated with construction date
    \item \textbf{Renovation history}: Older homes may have been renovated with lead-free materials, while some newer homes may contain lead from imported goods
    \item \textbf{Fuel loading scaling}: The dominant control on contamination may be total fuel mass rather than fuel composition, consistent with our observation that housing density correlates with Pb concentration
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{XRF Field Screening: Method Validation and Recommendations}

A primary objective of this study was to evaluate portable XRF as a field screening tool for WUI fire ash, addressing a gap in current post-fire assessment protocols. The high variability in XRF measurements for typical ash samples (R\textsuperscript{2} = 0.26 after outlier removal) presents challenges for field screening applications. The systematic overestimation (slope $\sim$3.9) combined with a positive intercept ($\sim$104 ppm) means that XRF will classify some samples as potentially hazardous that would test below the RSL by laboratory methods.

For conservative screening, this positive bias may be acceptable, as it errs on the side of caution. However, the dependence of bias on sample properties---particularly char content---introduces systematic errors that could lead to inconsistent classification. Darker, char-rich samples will tend to be under-flagged, while brighter samples will be over-flagged.

We recommend the following protocol for XRF field screening of wildfire ash:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item Apply the empirical correction: $\text{Pb}_{\text{est}} = (\text{Pb}_{\text{XRF}} - 104) / 3.9$
    \item Use an XRF threshold of 1,662 ppm as the primary screening criterion
    \item Visually assess sample brightness and adjust interpretation accordingly:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item Dark/char-rich samples: True contamination may be higher than XRF indicates
        \item Bright/ash-white samples: True contamination likely lower than XRF indicates
    \end{itemize}
    \item Confirm borderline samples (XRF 1,200--2,500 ppm) with laboratory analysis
\end{enumerate}

These correction factors are specific to the benchtop EDXRF instrument and ash matrix conditions in this study; portable handheld XRF units may require independent calibration. We also evaluated FTIR, ASD reflectance, and AVIRIS remote sensing as alternative or complementary matrix characterization approaches; XRF-derived organic content outperformed these spectral proxies for multi-element correction (Supplementary Materials).

\subsection{Implications for Post-Fire Remediation}

The spatial heterogeneity of contamination observed in this study---with 15\% of samples exceeding the EPA RSL while the majority fell below regulatory thresholds---has implications for remediation strategy. Soil scraping has been employed following the 2025 Los Angeles fires, with \citet{Flores2025} reporting that scraping reduced Pb concentrations in Altadena soils but had limited effect on As concentrations. Our identification of localized contamination hotspots suggests that targeted remediation of high-concentration areas may be more efficient than uniform treatment.

The location of contamination hotspots relative to sensitive receptors (residences, schools, recharge zones) should inform prioritization. Several samples exceeding RSL values were collected from areas overlying the Raymond Groundwater Basin recharge zone, where post-fire runoff could potentially transport metals to groundwater. While Pb mobility is generally limited at circumneutral pH, the thick unsaturated zone and engineered debris basins may attenuate contaminant transport. Long-term monitoring of groundwater quality in the Raymond Basin is warranted given the magnitude of this fire event and the basin's role in municipal water supply.

%==============================================================================
\section{Conclusions}
%==============================================================================

Analysis of 39 ash samples from the 2025 Eaton Fire reveals the following key findings:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Contamination levels}: Pb concentrations ranged from 14 to 18,528 ppm (median 61 ppm), with 15\% exceeding the EPA RSL of 400 ppm. The distribution is highly skewed with localized hot spots.

    \item \textbf{XRF correction}: Portable XRF systematically overestimates Pb in ash samples. The recommended correction is: $\text{Pb}_{\text{ICP-MS}} = (\text{Pb}_{\text{XRF}} - 104) / 3.9$. For field screening, use an XRF threshold of 1,662 ppm to identify samples likely exceeding the 400 ppm RSL.

    \item \textbf{Matrix effects}: Sample properties including mineralogy and char content introduce systematic bias in XRF measurements. Visual assessment of sample brightness can inform interpretation of XRF results.

    \item \textbf{Contamination predictors}: Housing density is positively correlated with Pb contamination, consistent with greater mass of combustible materials. Housing age (pre-1978 construction) is \textit{not} a significant predictor in this study area, possibly due to renovation history or non-paint lead sources.

    \item \textbf{Geogenic baseline}: Background Pb from San Gabriel Mountains-derived alluvium is 10--30 ppm. Median ash Pb (61 ppm) indicates widespread low-level anthropogenic enrichment.
\end{enumerate}

These findings demonstrate the complexity of post-fire contamination assessment and the utility of combining rapid XRF screening with ICP-MS validation for effective field characterization of WUI fire ash.

\section*{Supplementary Materials: Multi-Spectral Proxy Evaluation}

\subsection*{S1. Overview of Spectral Methods}

We evaluated three spectral approaches for characterizing WUI fire ash composition and predicting metal contamination: (1) Fourier-transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR) for organic functional group identification, (2) visible-near infrared (VNIR) reflectance spectroscopy using an ASD FieldSpec for optical properties, and (3) AVIRIS-3 airborne imaging spectroscopy for remote sensing applications. These methods were compared against XRF-derived compositional proxies to assess their utility for field screening and contamination prediction.

\subsection*{S2. FTIR Spectroscopy}

\subsubsection*{S2.1 Methods}

FTIR spectra were acquired on ground ash samples ($<$250 $\mu$m) using attenuated total reflectance (ATR) mode. Spectra were collected from 4000--400 cm$^{-1}$ at 4 cm$^{-1}$ resolution with 64 co-added scans. Band intensities were extracted for diagnostic functional groups including aliphatic C-H stretches (2920, 2850 cm$^{-1}$), carbonyl C=O (1720 cm$^{-1}$), aromatic C=C (1600 cm$^{-1}$), silicate Si-O (1080 cm$^{-1}$), and carbonate CO$_3$ (1420, 875 cm$^{-1}$).

\subsubsection*{S2.2 Results}

FTIR-derived organic proxies showed moderate correlations with XRF-ICP-MS offset magnitude (Table~\ref{tab:ftir_proxies}). The C-H stretch intensity at 2920 cm$^{-1}$ correlated with Pb offset (r = 0.50, p $<$ 0.01) but not with Zn or Cu offsets. However, FTIR band intensities exhibited severe multicollinearity (r $>$ 0.99 between most organic bands), indicating that they primarily reflect overall spectral intensity rather than independent chemical information. This limits their utility as quantitative predictors of specific matrix properties.

The CH/Silicate ratio, designed to normalize organic content against mineral matrix, showed improved but still modest correlations (mean $|$r$|$ = 0.28 across Pb, Zn, Cu). Char-specific indices derived from aromatic/aliphatic ratios showed weak correlations (r $<$ 0.1), suggesting that char content is not a primary driver of XRF matrix effects in these samples.

\subsection*{S3. ASD Reflectance Spectroscopy}

\subsubsection*{S3.1 Methods}

Reflectance spectra were measured using an ASD FieldSpec 4 spectrometer (350--2500 nm) with a contact probe under controlled illumination. Spectral indices were calculated including visible slope (450--700 nm), NIR slope (700--1300 nm), and overall albedo (char darkness proxy). These indices target optical properties associated with organic carbon content and combustion completeness.

\subsubsection*{S3.2 Results}

ASD-derived indices showed element-specific correlations with XRF offset (Table~\ref{tab:asd_proxies}). The visible slope correlated significantly with Pb offset (r = $-$0.49, p $<$ 0.05) but not with Zn or Cu, suggesting that darker samples (steeper negative slope, more char) have smaller Pb overestimation by XRF. The char darkness index showed weak positive correlation with Pb offset (r = 0.39), consistent with the visible slope finding.

The element-specific nature of ASD correlations limits their utility as universal matrix correction factors. Darker, char-rich samples may require different correction factors than brighter, mineral-dominated samples, but a single ASD index cannot simultaneously correct for multiple elements.

\subsection*{S4. AVIRIS-3 Remote Sensing}

\subsubsection*{S4.1 Data Acquisition}

AVIRIS-3 (Airborne Visible/Infrared Imaging Spectrometer) data were acquired over the Eaton Fire burn area on January 15, 2025. We extracted differenced Normalized Burn Ratio (dNBR) values and other spectral indices at sample locations to evaluate remote sensing proxies for metal contamination prediction.

\subsubsection*{S4.2 Results and Limitations}

Of the 39 ash samples with ICP-MS data, only 13 had coincident AVIRIS dNBR coverage, substantially limiting statistical power. Within this subset, dNBR showed a moderate positive correlation with Pb concentration (r = 0.38, p = 0.046), explaining approximately 15\% of Pb variance (Table~\ref{tab:aviris_assessment}).

Classification analysis using dNBR to predict high Pb ($>$200 mg/kg) samples yielded 67\% sensitivity and 100\% specificity at the optimal threshold, with positive and negative predictive values of 100\% and 78\%, respectively. While the correlation is statistically significant, the modest effect size and small sample size preclude confident operational deployment.

\subsubsection*{S4.3 Recommendations for Remote Sensing Applications}

Based on this assessment, dNBR alone is insufficient for operational contamination screening but may contribute to multi-variable prioritization models combining:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Burn severity indices (dNBR)
    \item Structure density from parcel data
    \item Pre-1978 housing age fractions
    \item Land use classification
\end{itemize}

Future studies should obtain full L1b hyperspectral radiance data to evaluate whether specific absorption features (e.g., metal-bearing minerals, organic matter) provide improved predictive capability beyond broadband indices.

\subsection*{S5. Comparison of Proxy Methods}

The XRF-derived organic proxy (100 $-$ sum of major oxides) outperformed both FTIR and ASD spectral proxies for predicting XRF-ICP-MS offset across all three elements (Table~\ref{tab:proxy_comparison}). This result is expected given that the XRF organic proxy directly estimates the mass fraction not accounted for by crystalline mineral phases, which is the primary source of matrix effects in XRF measurements.

\textbf{Key findings:}
\begin{enumerate}
    \item FTIR proxies suffer from severe multicollinearity, limiting independent predictive value
    \item ASD proxies show element-specific responses, precluding universal correction factors
    \item XRF-derived organic content provides the most robust multi-element correction
    \item AVIRIS dNBR shows promise but requires validation with larger sample sizes
\end{enumerate}

For practical field deployment, we recommend using XRF organic content (calculated as 100 $-$ oxide sum) as the primary matrix correction parameter, with visual assessment of sample brightness (char content) as a qualitative modifier.
