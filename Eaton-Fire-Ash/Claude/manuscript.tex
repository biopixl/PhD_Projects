\begin{abstract}
%==============================================================================

Wildland-urban interface (WUI) fires generate ash deposits with elevated heavy metal concentrations derived from combusted building materials, consumer products, and vehicles. The January 2025 Eaton Fire in Los Angeles County destroyed over 9,400 structures in the foothill communities of Altadena and Pasadena, depositing ash across residential areas overlying the Raymond Groundwater Basin. We quantified metal contamination in 39 ash samples using inductively coupled plasma mass spectrometry (ICP-MS) and evaluated portable X-ray fluorescence (XRF) as a field screening method. Lead concentrations ranged from 14 to 18,528 mg/kg (median 61 mg/kg), with 15\% of samples exceeding the U.S. Environmental Protection Agency residential screening level of 400 mg/kg. Additional exceedances were observed for cobalt (n=5), copper (n=1), and zinc (n=1). XRF measurements exhibited matrix-dependent bias correlated with ash mineralogy and organic carbon content, requiring empirical correction for quantitative interpretation. 

\end{abstract}


\section{Introduction}


Expansion of the wildland-urban interface (WUI) across the western United States has increased both the frequency of structure-involved wildfires and the magnitude of associated environmental contamination \citep{Radeloff2018}. Combustion of residential and commercial structures generates ash with metal concentrations substantially exceeding those of wildland fire ash, which primarily reflects the geochemistry of vegetation and underlying soils \citep{Plumlee2007, Quarles2020}. Structure fire ash contains lead (Pb) from legacy paint, pipes, and solder; copper (Cu) and zinc (Zn) from electrical infrastructure and galvanized metals; and chromium (Cr) from plating and pressure-treated lumber \citep{Plumlee2007, Gabos2023}.

Recent WUI fire studies have documented Pb concentrations in structure ash ranging from tens to tens of thousands of mg/kg, with substantial spatial heterogeneity reflecting the distribution of Pb-bearing materials within individual properties \citep{Plumlee2007, Hubbs2023}. The 2021 Marshall Fire in Colorado and 2018 Camp Fire in California both generated widespread ash deposits requiring extensive characterization and remediation \citep{Gabos2023, Hubbs2023}.

The January 2025 Eaton Fire in Los Angeles County destroyed over 9,400 structures across 14,117 acres (5,714 ha) in the foothill communities of Altadena and Pasadena. The distribution of ash across residential areas, parks, and recharge zones for the Raymond Groundwater Basin necessitated rapid characterization of contamination levels and spatial patterns.

Laboratory-based methods including inductively coupled plasma mass spectrometry (ICP-MS) provide accurate metal quantification but require substantial time and resources for sample processing. Portable X-ray fluorescence (XRF) spectrometry offers a rapid field alternative; however, matrix effects in heterogeneous materials such as wildfire ash introduce measurement bias requiring empirical correction \citep{Kalnicky2001, Weindorf2014}.

This study characterizes metal contamination in Eaton Fire ash deposits, evaluates XRF as a field screening method with empirical correction factors, and assesses the spatial distribution of contamination relative to regulatory screening levels.

%==============================================================================
\section{Study Area}
%==============================================================================

The Eaton Fire ignited on January 7, 2025 in Eaton Canyon at the base of the San Gabriel Mountains in Altadena, California (Figure~\ref{fig:study_area}). Santa Ana wind conditions with gusts exceeding 40 m/s drove rapid fire spread, with the perimeter expanding to 4,300 ha within 24 hours. The fire ultimately burned 5,714 ha and destroyed 9,418 structures before containment on January 31, 2025, causing 19 fatalities and ranking as the second most destructive wildfire in California history.

The burn area encompasses the communities of Altadena and portions of Pasadena at elevations of 360--550 m above sea level (34.127--34.207°N, 118.020--118.194°W). Residential development in the study area occurred predominantly between 1920 and 1970 \citep{Manson2022}, corresponding to the period of widespread lead-based paint application (discontinued 1978) and leaded gasoline use (phased out 1996), both recognized sources of legacy Pb contamination in urban soils \citep{Mielke1999, Laidlaw2012}.

%------------------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Geological Setting}
%------------------------------------------------------------------------------

The study area occupies the Raymond Basin at the base of the San Gabriel Mountains, which comprise Precambrian gneiss and Mesozoic granitic rocks uplifted along the Sierra Madre--Cucamonga fault zone \citep{Yerkes1985, Morton2014}. Alluvial fan deposits derived from Eaton Wash and adjacent drainages include Holocene unconsolidated sediments and Pleistocene semi-consolidated materials \citep{Morton2014}.

Granitic basement rocks in the region contain Pb at 10--30 mg/kg, Cu at 10--50 mg/kg, Zn at 40--80 mg/kg, and As at $<$5 mg/kg, consistent with average upper continental crust \citep{Rudnick2003}. Ultramafic lithologies, where present, may contain elevated Cr and Ni concentrations \citep{Oze2004}. Urban soils in the Los Angeles Basin typically exhibit Pb concentrations of 50--200 mg/kg reflecting legacy contamination from leaded gasoline and paint \citep{Mielke2011, Laidlaw2012}.

%------------------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Hydrogeological Context}
%------------------------------------------------------------------------------

The Raymond Basin is a 104 km\textsuperscript{2} alluvial aquifer system bounded by the San Gabriel Mountain front to the north, the Raymond Fault to the south, and the Arroyo Seco to the west \citep{USGS2018}. The aquifer comprises unconsolidated to semi-consolidated deposits with average thickness of 170 m and provides municipal water supply to Pasadena and adjacent communities. Recharge occurs primarily through infiltration in Eaton Wash and Arroyo Seco spreading basins \citep{USGS2018}. The distribution of fire-derived ash across these recharge zones raised concern for potential groundwater quality impacts.

\section{Methods}
%==============================================================================

\subsection{Sample Collection}

Ash samples were collected between January 16--25, 2025, approximately 9--18 days post-ignition and prior to the first significant rainfall on January 26, 2025. A 2.6 $\times$ 2.6 km grid was established for spatial planning, with sampling sites selected based on (1) public accessibility, (2) visible ash accumulation, and (3) absence of post-fire disturbance from remediation activities.

Field teams from the California Institute of Technology (n=74) and Jet Propulsion Laboratory (n=95) collected 169 samples within and adjacent to the burn perimeter, supplemented by 52 samples from community participants using standardized collection protocols. At each site, 5--15 mL of ash was collected from wind-deposited accumulations on impervious surfaces (sidewalks, curbs, driveways). At each sample site, we identified where ash was concentrated and collected 5-15 mL of ash. The JPL team used a small nylon-bristle paint brush and plastic laboratory weight boat and Caltech teams sampled using a wooden spoon and tweezers. For each sample, the teams collected both coarser ash flakes as well as fine soot and dust to achieve a representative sample. Note that while we attempted to only collect burned material, contamination with background dust was impossible to avoid. We also avoided locations where surfaces had been swept, washed, or otherwise altered following the fire event. In some grid cell locations where ash was plentiful, 2-3 samples were taken tens to a few hundred meters apart. Each sample was immediately transferred to a sample vial and the sample identifier, location, person sampling, date, and other miscellaneous notes. 

Community science participants were provided with standardized collection kits including a sampling bag, spoon, and wipe for ash collection. Submitted samples were registered and visually assessed to qualify the sample quantity for appropriate experimental analysis in the lab.

\subsection{Laboratory Analysis}

\subsubsection{Sample Preparation}
We selected a subset of samples to continue analysis as the ICP-MS procedure was too time and resource intensive to analyze all ash samples. We picked a subset that spanned the region (including samples from within the burn scar, downwind to the south, and on the eastern and western flanks). After picking this subset, we split each sample into subsamples for downstream analyses. To obtain representative subsamples from the bulk ash and debris collections, we employed a coning and quartering method on a clean, aluminum-covered bench surface. Each sample was first poured gently onto a large, ceramic plate. Using sanitized plastic scrapers, the material was formed into a central cone, homogenized, and then flattened into a disc. The disc was divided into four equal quarters, and two opposite quarters were recombined, re-coned, and quartered again. This iterative process was repeated until an estimated 1-3g of ash was obtained for downstream processing by grinding and sieving, while the remaining sample fraction was preserved as collected in-situ. Hereafter, we describe the analysis on the subsample processed with grinding and sieving. 

The resulting subsample was transferred onto folded weighing paper and loaded into a clean, labeled microcentrifuge tube with a unique identifier. Each subsample was weighed in a designated clean weighing room using a precision balance, and the mass was recorded. Subsamples were then stored in a dry, dark environment at ambient temperature. 

To standardize sample particle size and remove coarse debris, a second preparation step was carried out under a fume hood with appropriate personal protective equipment and clean lab practices. All tools and surfaces were pre-cleaned with 70\% ethanol, pressurized air, and lint-free wipes (Kimwipes) to minimize contamination. 

The ash subsample was poured into a ceramic mortar and photographed to document particle size and color before processing. Using a ceramic pestle, the ash was ground along the interior surface of the mortar until reduced to a uniform fine powder. 

Ground ash was transferred onto a folded square of weighing paper and returned to its labeled tube for sieving. A clean, square-cut mesh sieve (250 µm) was then mounted between the sample tube and a clean collection tube with two caps that had been punctured and glued together for containing all ash residue within the sample tubes. The sample was inverted and gently rotated to allow finer particles to pass through the mesh, separating the sample into two size fractions: (1) <250 µm, and (2) > 250 µm. Final masses for both fractions were recorded in the weighing room to assess recovery and particle distribution in the fractions. The <250 µm ash subsample was used for laboratory analysis with ICPMS and XRF to limit noise from large, refractory ash species and capture the contributions from the most mobile and bioaccessible ash species. 


\subsubsection{ICP-MS Analysis}

Samples were digested using EPA Method 3051A (microwave-assisted acid digestion) and analyzed by inductively coupled plasma mass spectrometry (ICP-MS) for a suite of 50+ elements including Pb, As, Cd, Cu, Zn, Cr, Ni, and rare earth elements (REEs). Detection limits were typically <0.1 ppm for most trace metals. Quality assurance included analysis of certified reference materials (CRMs), method blanks, and duplicate samples at a frequency of 10\%.

\subsubsection{XRF Analysis}

 XRF measurements were made using a benchtop energy-dispersive X-ray Fluorescence (EDXRF) spectrometer (INAM Expert 3L, INAM Scientific, Iran). The instrument is equipped with a high-resolution Silicon Drift Detector (SDD) and an X-ray tube source (Rh anode, 50 kV max voltage, 1000 µA max current), enabling detection of elements from Na (Z=11) to U (Z=92). Measurements were performed under vacuum to enhance sensitivity for light elements. Spectra were acquired over a 10-minute live time per sample with automatic peak deconvolution and matrix correction via the instrument’s fundamental parameters (FP) software package. Calibration for lead quantification was evaluated using kaolinite clay reference materials doped with a lead nitrate salt solution. Quantitative results were reported in mass fraction (\%) as independent elemental abundances, with oxide equivalents calculated post-acquisition for reporting purposes.

\subsection{Statistical Analysis}

All statistical analyses were performed in Python using scipy, numpy, and scikit-learn libraries. Spearman rank correlations were used to assess relationships between metal concentrations and potential predictor variables, as metal distributions were non-normal. Linear regression was used to develop XRF correction models, with residual analysis to identify matrix effects. Significance was assessed at $\alpha$ = 0.05 with Bonferroni correction applied for multiple comparisons where appropriate.

\subsection{Metal Contamination Levels}

The distribution was strongly right-skewed, with a small number of highly contaminated samples driving the elevated mean. Fifteen percent of samples exceeded the EPA residential soil screening level (RSL) of 400 ppm for Pb.

Using California's more protective 80 mg/kg residential screening level for Pb, 11 samples (28\%) exceeded this threshold. Seven samples (18\%) exceeded the 155 ppm level used in the Exide facility cleanup, and 6 samples (15\%) exceeded the EPA RSL of 400 ppm (Figure~\ref{fig:metal_concentrations}).

\subsection{XRF-ICP-MS Correction Model}

Comparison of  XRF and ICP-MS measurements revealed systematic overestimation of Pb by XRF (Figure~\ref{fig:xrf_correction}). Using all data (n = 37), the linear relationship was:

\begin{equation}
\text{Pb}_{\text{XRF}} = 1.71 \times \text{Pb}_{\text{ICP-MS}} + 337 \quad (R^2 = 0.98)
\end{equation}

However, this model was heavily influenced by extreme outliers. After removing 7 statistical outliers identified by the IQR method (samples with Pb > 134 ppm by ICP-MS or XRF > 805 ppm), the relationship for typical samples (n = 30) was:

\begin{equation}
\text{Pb}_{\text{XRF}} = 3.90 \times \text{Pb}_{\text{ICP-MS}} + 104 \quad (R^2 = 0.26)
\end{equation}

The substantially lower R² for the cleaned dataset indicates high variability in XRF measurements for typical ash samples, likely due to matrix effects. The practical correction formula for converting XRF field measurements to ICP-MS equivalent values is:

\begin{equation}
\text{Pb}_{\text{ICP-MS}} \approx \frac{\text{Pb}_{\text{XRF}} - 104}{3.9}
\end{equation}

For field screening purposes, the XRF threshold corresponding to the EPA RSL of 400 ppm is:

\begin{equation}
\text{Pb}_{\text{XRF, threshold}} = 3.90 \times 400 + 104 = 1,662 \text{ ppm}
\end{equation}

\section{Discussion}
%==============================================================================

\subsection{Contamination Sources and Patterns}

The wide range of Pb concentrations observed (14--18,528 ppm) reflects the heterogeneous nature of structure fire ash, where localized ``hot spots'' associated with specific materials (e.g., painted surfaces, batteries, lead pipes) occur within a matrix of lower-contamination ash. The median concentration of 61 ppm is below the EPA RSL of 400 ppm but exceeds typical geogenic background levels of 10--30 ppm, indicating widespread low-level anthropogenic enrichment even in samples not classified as hazardous.

The lack of correlation between Pb and pre-1978 housing age is surprising given the well-documented association between older housing and lead contamination in urban soils \citep{Mielke1999}. Several factors may explain this finding:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Combustion homogenization}: Fire may redistribute and mix materials from different construction eras within a neighborhood
    \item \textbf{Non-paint sources}: Lead in structure fire ash may derive substantially from non-paint sources (pipes, solder, electronics) not strictly correlated with construction date
    \item \textbf{Renovation history}: Older homes may have been renovated with lead-free materials, while some newer homes may contain lead from imported goods
    \item \textbf{Spatial confounding}: Housing age may be correlated with other factors (e.g., lot size, socioeconomic status) that influence contamination patterns
\end{enumerate}

The significant correlation between housing density and Pb supports a ``mass of materials'' interpretation, where contamination scales with the total amount of combustible materials regardless of specific sources.

\subsection{XRF Limitations and Recommendations}

The high variability in XRF measurements for typical ash samples (R² = 0.26 after outlier removal) presents challenges for field screening applications. The systematic overestimation (slope $\sim$3.9) combined with a positive intercept ($\sim$104 ppm) means that XRF will classify some samples as potentially hazardous that would test below the RSL by laboratory methods.

For conservative screening, this positive bias may be acceptable, as it errs on the side of caution. However, the dependence of bias on sample properties---particularly char content---introduces systematic errors that could lead to inconsistent classification. Darker, char-rich samples will tend to be under-flagged, while brighter samples will be over-flagged.

We recommend the following protocol for XRF field screening of wildfire ash:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item Apply the empirical correction: $\text{Pb}_{\text{est}} = (\text{Pb}_{\text{XRF}} - 104) / 3.9$
    \item Use an XRF threshold of 1,662 ppm as the primary screening criterion
    \item Visually assess sample brightness and adjust interpretation accordingly:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item Dark/char-rich samples: True contamination may be higher than XRF indicates
        \item Bright/ash-white samples: True contamination likely lower than XRF indicates
    \end{itemize}
    \item Confirm borderline samples (XRF 1,200--2,500 ppm) with laboratory analysis
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Groundwater Vulnerability}

The location of the Eaton Fire burn area over the Raymond Groundwater Basin raises concerns about potential metal transport to groundwater through post-fire runoff and infiltration. The spreading basins along Eaton Wash and the Arroyo Seco that provide primary recharge to the basin could receive contaminated sediment during winter storms. Given that the Raymond Basin supplies approximately one-third of Pasadena's water supply, monitoring of recharge water quality is warranted.

However, several factors may limit groundwater impacts:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Lead is relatively immobile in soite environments at circumneutral pH
    \item The thick unsaturated zone (tens of meters) provides opportunity for attenuation
    \item Engineered debris basins may capture contaminated sediment before it reaches spreading grounds
\end{itemize}

Long-term monitoring of groundwater Pb concentrations downgradient of the burn area will be necessary to assess actual impacts.

%==============================================================================
\section{Conclusions}
%==============================================================================

Analysis of 39 ash samples from the 2025 Eaton Fire reveals the following key findings:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Contamination levels}: Pb concentrations ranged from 14 to 18,528 ppm (median 61 ppm), with 15\% exceeding the EPA RSL of 400 ppm. The distribution is highly skewed with localized hot spots.

    \item \textbf{XRF correction}: Portable XRF systematically overestimates Pb in ash samples. The recommended correction is: $\text{Pb}_{\text{ICP-MS}} = (\text{Pb}_{\text{XRF}} - 104) / 3.9$. For field screening, use an XRF threshold of 1,662 ppm to identify samples likely exceeding the 400 ppm RSL.

    \item \textbf{Matrix effects}: Sample properties including mineralogy and char content introduce systematic bias in XRF measurements. Visual assessment of sample brightness can inform interpretation of XRF results.

    \item \textbf{Contamination predictors}: Housing density is positively correlated with Pb contamination, consistent with greater mass of combustible materials. Housing age (pre-1978 construction) is \textit{not} a significant predictor in this study area, possibly due to renovation history or non-paint lead sources.

    \item \textbf{Geogenic baseline}: Background Pb from San Gabriel Mountains-derived alluvium is 10--30 ppm. Median ash Pb (61 ppm) indicates widespread low-level anthropogenic enrichment.
\end{enumerate}

These findings demonstrate the complexity of post-fire contamination assessment and the utility of combining rapid XRF screening with ICP-MS validation for effective field characterization of WUI fire ash.
