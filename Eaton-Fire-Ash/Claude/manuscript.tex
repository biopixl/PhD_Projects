\begin{abstract}
%==============================================================================

Wildland-urban interface (WUI) fires release ash deposits with elevated heavy metal concentrations derived from combusted building materials, consumer products, and vehicles. The January 2025 Eaton Fire in Los Angeles County destroyed over 9,400 structures in the foothill communities of Altadena and Pasadena, depositing ash across residential areas in Los Angeles. We quantified metal contamination in 39 ash samples using inductively coupled plasma mass spectrometry (ICP-MS) and evaluated X-ray fluorescence (XRF) as a field screening method. Lead concentrations ranged from 14 to 18,528 mg/kg (median 61 mg/kg), with 15\% of samples exceeding the U.S. Environmental Protection Agency residential screening level of 400 mg/kg. Additional exceedances were observed for cobalt (n=5), copper (n=1), and zinc (n=1). XRF measurements exhibited matrix-dependent bias correlated with ash mineralogy and organic carbon content, requiring empirical correction for quantitative interpretation. 

\end{abstract}


\section{Introduction}

\subsection{Ash from Wildland-Urban Interface Fires}
Coarse, residual ash from fires at the wildland-urban interface (WUI) is produced by incomplete combustion of complex fuel mixtures. Pre-fire land cover in the WUI includes both wildland and urban vegetation biomass, as well as a gradient of infrastructure development containing economic metals and metal-treated products. Expansion of the WUI across the western United States has increased both the frequency of structure-involved wildfires and the magnitude of associated environmental contamination \cite{Radeloff2018}. Combustion of residential and commercial structures generates ash with metal concentrations substantially exceeding those of wildland fire ash, which primarily reflects the composition of vegetation and underlying soils \textbackslash{}citep\{Plumlee2007, Quarles2020\}. The 2021 Marshall Fire in Colorado and the 2018 Camp Fire in California both generated widespread ash deposits requiring extensive characterization and remediation \textbackslash{}citep\{Gabos2023, Hubbs2023\}. 

\subsection{The 2025 Eaton Fire}
The January 2025 Eaton Fire ignited in a county park reserve on the evening of January 7, 2025 adjacent to the Southern San Gabriel Mountains and WUI communities of Altadena, Pasadena, Sierra Madre, and La Canada Flintridge. An extreme Santa Ana wind event occurring concurrent with the fire storm event produced wind gusts up to 90 mph recorded at the Eaton Canyon site of ignition. The fire perimeter had exploded to 10,600+ acres by the next day with most of the catastrophic damage occurring in the first 36 hours \cite{Schissel2025}. No progress on containment was reported until January 12 as the fire perimeter progressed northward into the San Gabriel Mountain wilderness preserves. For three weeks the fire remained below 100\% containment destroying over 9,000 structures across approximately 14,000 acres (5,670 ha) before full containment on January 31, 2025 \cite{CALFIRE2025}. The fire caused 19 confirmed fatalities and became the fifth deadliest and second most destructive wildfire in California history \cite{CALFIRE2025}. The widespread distribution of potentially contaminated ash across residential neighborhoods, parks, and recharge zones for the Raymond Groundwater Basin created an urgent need for rapid assessment of contamination levels and spatial patterns. 

\subsection{Geological Setting}
The study area occupies the Raymond Basin at the base of the San Gabriel Mountains, which comprise Precambrian gneiss and Mesozoic granitic rocks uplifted along the Sierra Madre--Cucamonga fault zone \\\cite{Yerkes1985, Morton2014}. Alluvial fan deposits derived from Eaton Wash and adjacent drainages include Holocene unconsolidated sediments and Pleistocene semi-consolidated materials \cite{Morton2014}. Minor ultramafic lithologies, including serpentinite, occur in the eastern San Gabriel Mountains and can contribute trace metals to alluvial sediments \cite{USGS2019, Fendorf2023}. More than 90\% of homes in Altadena were built before 1975\cite{USCensus2020}, during the period of widespread lead-based paint use (banned 1978) and leaded gasoline emissions (phased out 1996) \cite{Mielke1999}. The broader Los Angeles Basin has documented legacy contamination from industrial sources, including the Exide battery recycling facility approximately 20 km south of the study area, which contaminated thousands of properties via atmospheric deposition \cite{DTSC2020}.

\section{Methods}
%==============================================================================

\subsection{Sample Collection}

Ash samples were collected between January 16-25, 2025, approximately 9-18 days post-ignition and prior to the first significant rainfall on January 26, 2025. A 2.6 $\times$ 2.6 km grid was established for spatial planning, with sampling sites selected based on (1) public accessibility, (2) visible ash accumulation, and (3) absence of post-fire disturbance from remediation activities.

Field teams from the California Institute of Technology (n=74) and Jet Propulsion Laboratory (n=95) collected 169 samples within and adjacent to the burn perimeter, supplemented by 52 samples from community volunteers using standardized collection protocols. At each site, ash was collected from wind-deposited accumulations on impervious surfaces (sidewalks, curbs, driveways). At each sample site, we identified where ash was concentrated and collected 5-15 mL of ash. The JPL team used a small nylon-bristle paint brush and plastic laboratory weight boat and Caltech teams sampled using a wooden spoon and tweezers. For each sample, the teams collected both coarser ash flakes as well as fine soot and dust to achieve a representative sample. Note that while we attempted to only collect burned material, contamination with background dust was impossible to avoid. We also avoided locations where surfaces had been swept, washed, or otherwise altered following the fire event. In some grid cell locations where ash was plentiful, 2-3 samples were taken tens to a few hundred meters apart. Each sample was immediately transferred to a sample vial and the sample identifier, location, person sampling, date, and other miscellaneous notes. 

Community science volunteers were provided with standardized collection kits including a sampling bag, spoon, and wipe for ash collection. Submitted samples were registered and visually assessed to qualify the sample quantity for appropriate experimental analysis in the lab.

\subsection{Laboratory Analysis}

\subsubsection{Sample Preparation}
We selected a subset of samples to continue analysis as the ICP-MS procedure was too time and resource intensive to analyze all ash samples. We picked a subset that spanned the region (including samples from within the burn scar, downwind to the south, and on the eastern and western flanks). After picking this subset, we split each sample into subsamples for downstream analyses. To obtain representative subsamples from the bulk ash and debris collections, we employed a coning and quartering method on a clean, aluminum-covered bench surface. Each sample was first poured gently onto a large, ceramic plate. Using sanitized plastic scrapers, the material was formed into a central cone, homogenized, and then flattened into a disc. The disc was divided into four equal quarters, and two opposite quarters were recombined, re-coned, and quartered again. This iterative process was repeated until an estimated 1-3g of ash was obtained for downstream processing by grinding and sieving, while the remaining sample fraction was preserved as collected in-situ. Hereafter, we describe the analysis on the subsample processed with grinding and sieving. 

The resulting subsample was transferred onto folded weighing paper and loaded into a clean, labeled microcentrifuge tube with a unique identifier. Each subsample was weighed in a designated clean weighing room using a precision balance, and the mass was recorded. Subsamples were then stored in a dry, dark environment at ambient temperature. 

To standardize sample particle size and remove coarse debris, a second preparation step was carried out under a fume hood with appropriate personal protective equipment and clean lab practices. All tools and surfaces were pre-cleaned with 70\% ethanol, pressurized air, and lint-free wipes (Kimwipes) to minimize contamination. 

The ash subsample was poured into a ceramic mortar and photographed to document particle size and color before processing. Using a ceramic pestle, the ash was ground along the interior surface of the mortar until reduced to a uniform fine powder. 

Ground ash was transferred onto a folded square of weighing paper and returned to its labeled tube for sieving. A clean, square-cut mesh sieve (250 µm) was then mounted between the sample tube and a clean collection tube with two caps that had been punctured and adhered together for containing all ash residue within the sample tubes. The sample was inverted and gently rotated to allow finer particles to pass through the mesh, separating the sample into two size fractions: (1) <250 µm, and (2) > 250 µm. Final masses for both fractions were recorded in the weighing room to assess recovery and particle distribution in the fractions. The <250 µm ash subsample was used for laboratory analysis with ICPMS and XRF to limit noise from large, refractory ash species and capture the contributions from the most mobile and bioaccessible ash species. 


\subsubsection{ICP-MS Analysis}

Samples were digested using EPA Method 3051A (microwave-assisted acid digestion) and analyzed by inductively coupled plasma mass spectrometry (ICP-MS) for a suite of 50+ elements including Pb, As, Cd, Cu, Zn, Cr, Ni, and rare earth elements (REEs). Detection limits were typically <0.1 ppm for most trace metals. Quality assurance included analysis of certified reference materials (CRMs), method blanks, and duplicate samples at a frequency of 10\%.

\subsubsection{XRF Analysis}

 XRF measurements were made using a benchtop energy-dispersive X-ray Fluorescence (EDXRF) spectrometer (INAM Expert 3L, INAM Scientific, Iran). The instrument is equipped with a high-resolution Silicon Drift Detector (SDD) and an X-ray tube source (Rh anode, 50 kV max voltage, 1000 µA max current), enabling detection of elements from Na (Z=11) to U (Z=92). Measurements were performed under vacuum to enhance sensitivity for light elements. Spectra were acquired over a 10-minute live time per sample with automatic peak deconvolution and matrix correction via the instrument’s fundamental parameters (FP) software package. Calibration for lead quantification was evaluated using kaolinite clay reference materials doped with a lead nitrate salt solution. Quantitative results were reported in mass fraction (\%) as independent elemental abundances, with oxide equivalents calculated post-acquisition for reporting purposes.

\subsection{Statistical Analysis}

All statistical analyses were performed in Python using scipy, numpy, and scikit-learn libraries. Spearman rank correlations were used to assess relationships between metal concentrations and potential predictor variables, as metal distributions were non-normal. Linear regression was used to develop XRF correction models, with residual analysis to identify matrix effects. Significance was assessed at $\alpha$ = 0.05 with Bonferroni correction applied for multiple comparisons where appropriate.

\section{Results and Discussion}
%==============================================================================

\subsection{Metals in Eaton Fire Ashes}

Lead (Pb) concentrations in ash samples ranged from 14 to 18,528 ppm with a median of 61 ppm and mean of 638 ppm (Table~\ref{tab:metal_summary}). The distribution was strongly right-skewed, with a small number of highly contaminated samples driving the elevated mean. Fifteen percent of samples exceeded the EPA residential soil screening level (RSL) of 400 ppm for Pb.

Using California's more protective 80 mg/kg residential screening level for Pb, 11 samples (28\%) exceeded this threshold. Seven samples (18\%) exceeded the 155 ppm level used in the Exide facility cleanup, and 6 samples (15\%) exceeded the EPA RSL of 400 ppm, and 2 samples exceed the EPA Hazardous waste threshold (Figure~\ref{fig:metal_concentrations}).

\subsection{Contamination Sources and Patterns}

The wide range of Pb concentrations observed (14--18,528 ppm) reflects the heterogeneous nature of structure fire ash, where localized ``hot spots'' associated with specific materials (e.g., painted surfaces, batteries, lead pipes) occur within a matrix of lower-contamination ash. The median concentration of 61 ppm is below the EPA RSL of 400 ppm but exceeds typical geogenic background levels of 10--30 ppm, indicating widespread low-level anthropogenic enrichment even in samples not classified as hazardous.

The lack of correlation between Pb and pre-1978 housing age is surprising given the well-documented association between older housing and lead contamination in urban soils \citep{Mielke1999}. Several factors may explain this finding:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Combustion homogenization}: Fire may redistribute and mix materials from different construction eras within a neighborhood
    \item \textbf{Non-paint sources}: Lead in structure fire ash may derive substantially from non-paint sources (pipes, solder, electronics) not strictly correlated with construction date
    \item \textbf{Renovation history}: Older homes may have been renovated with lead-free materials, while some newer homes may contain lead from imported goods
    \item \textbf{Spatial confounding}: Housing age may be correlated with other factors (e.g., lot size, socioeconomic status) that influence contamination patterns
\end{enumerate}

The significant correlation between housing density and Pb supports a ``mass of materials'' interpretation, where contamination scales with the total amount of combustible materials regardless of specific sources.


%==============================================================================
\section{Conclusions}
%==============================================================================

Analysis of 39 ash samples from the 2025 Eaton Fire reveals the following key findings:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Contamination levels}: Pb concentrations ranged from 14 to 18,528 ppm (median 61 ppm), with 15\% exceeding the EPA RSL of 400 ppm. The distribution is highly skewed with localized hot spots.

    \item \textbf{XRF correction}: Portable XRF systematically overestimates Pb in ash samples. The recommended correction is: $\text{Pb}_{\text{ICP-MS}} = (\text{Pb}_{\text{XRF}} - 104) / 3.9$. For field screening, use an XRF threshold of 1,662 ppm to identify samples likely exceeding the 400 ppm RSL.

    \item \textbf{Matrix effects}: Sample properties including mineralogy and char content introduce systematic bias in XRF measurements. Visual assessment of sample brightness can inform interpretation of XRF results.

    \item \textbf{Contamination predictors}: Housing density is positively correlated with Pb contamination, consistent with greater mass of combustible materials. Housing age (pre-1978 construction) is \textit{not} a significant predictor in this study area, possibly due to renovation history or non-paint lead sources.

    \item \textbf{Geogenic baseline}: Background Pb from San Gabriel Mountains-derived alluvium is 10--30 ppm. Median ash Pb (61 ppm) indicates widespread low-level anthropogenic enrichment.
\end{enumerate}

These findings demonstrate the complexity of post-fire contamination assessment and the utility of combining rapid XRF screening with ICP-MS validation for effective field characterization of WUI fire ash.
